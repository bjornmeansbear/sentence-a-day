## Course Description

Students will learn the tools of the human-centred design process while tackling a contemporary and relevant issue each semester. The HCD process includes site-visits and in-person interviews to gather insights, structured synthesis of those insights into actionable opportunities for form-giving, directed and divergent brainstorm, prototyping and feedback loops for iterative refinement, and creating engaging narrative documentation to communicate novel design solutions developed from the initial insights.

Course type: Studio
Credits: 3
Modality: In Person

### What this course is

This course spends a semester on one real place: the Hilton Baltimore Inner Harbor, a 757-room hotel owned by the City of Baltimore. We'll go there six times. You will talk to people who work there and people staying there, and you will design in response to what you find rather than what you assumed before you went.

The first half is team research. You go out, observe, interview, and bring everything back to the group — there are no private findings in this course. The second half is individual, but the work keeps returning to the room to be questioned.

Three things make this different from a studio where you invent your own brief.

The work is about systems, not objects. A change in a hotel lobby moves staff labor, guest traffic, and resource use with it. You will be asked repeatedly what your design does to everything around it, and "it looks good" is not an answer to that question.

We work in physical materials, in the room, together. Cardboard, paper, foam core, marker. No air-comps. No designing alone on a laptop while the group waits. No dividing the work so nobody has to talk to anybody.

Most of what you make will fail, on purpose. A rough prototype that a hotel guest tells you won't work has done its job. This course is graded on the process you can show — your research, your iterations, and what you learned when something broke — not on arriving at a finished solution.

You do not need prior research experience. The first weeks are structured: specific observation prompts, a set number of interviews, named methods to follow. The brief opens up as you build ground to stand on.

You'll be working alongside D&I students from every co-major. By December, the goal is that you identify a little less by your co-major and a little more as a designer who can be dropped into an unfamiliar problem and be useful.

## Course Structure/Format

### Duration

A traditional fall semester: **Aug 24 – Dec 7**, sixteen weeks. We meet fourteen times — MICA is closed Sep 7 (Labor Day) and Oct 12 (fall break) for our class meetings.

### The two halves

**Weeks 1–8 — team research.** You work in teams. You split up interviews, go to the site, and report back. There are no private findings; everything one person learns belongs to the whole group. The research follows four moves, and we name which one we're on each week:

- **Learn** — look things up. Traditional research, before you go anywhere.
- **Look** — go to the site and observe. No talking required.
- **Ask** — interview people where they actually are.
- **Try** — make something and find out what it teaches you.

**Weeks 9–16 — individual work.** You take one prompt out of the shared research and build toward it on your own. The work still comes back to the group every week to be questioned, and the standard doesn't change: you're designing an intervention in a system, not an object by itself.

### Where we meet

- **Campus** — Dolphin 300, nine of our fourteen sessions.
- **Hilton Baltimore Inner Harbor** — 401 W Pratt St. Five sessions: Aug 31, Sep 14, Oct 5, Nov 2, Nov 16. We travel as a group; Light rail transit is covered.
- **Dec 7** — final presentations, location confirmed by early November.

### How a day runs

Six hours is a long time. A campus Monday usually runs: open at 9:00 with what happened since last week and what's stuck; a short lecture or demo, then working until noon; lunch; a second working block, crit, or workshop until 2:30; pin up and close by 3:00.

A Hilton Monday runs differently — we travel together, work on the property most of the day, and debrief before heading back. You get a plan the week before each site visit. Bring what's on the list; you can't run back to the studio for a marker.

### Between Mondays

We meet once a week, so six days pass between sessions and a lot of the actual work happens in that gap. Expect **at least 3–6 hours of work outside class each week** — reading, fieldwork, building, documenting. It's not evenly distributed: site-visit weeks and the weeks before a test are heavier.

Because we meet once a week, one absence leaves a two-week hole. See Attendance.

### Canvas

Everything lives in Canvas.

- **Readings** — PDFs and links, in the week they're assigned.
- **Slides and handouts** — posted after each session.
- **Weekly documentation** — Weeks 1–13 you post to a **Discussion**, not a private assignment. Your process is visible to the class on purpose; this course runs on shared notes and weekly crits. Weeks 14–16 switch to private assignments, since the final work and self-reflection are yours.
- **Attendance** — recorded in Canvas each session.
- **Gradebook** — organized by skill area (Research & Fieldwork, Synthesis, Prototyping & Iteration, Feedback & Testing, Final Communication), not by week. Your grade reflects what you can do, not which boxes you checked when.

The weekly documentation post uses the **same three fields every time**: photos of what you made, a short reflection (what did you build or test, what did it teach you, what's still unanswered), and — in weeks with an assigned reading — one specific change you made to your work because of that reading.

## Assessment/Grading

### The short version

**Most weeks, you are graded on whether you did the work, not on whether it looks good.**

A rough cardboard mockup that taught you something is a better submission than a polished one that taught you nothing. If you play it safe to protect a grade, you'll learn less and score the same. So don't.

There are two exceptions, and they're deliberate.

### Three phases, graded differently

**Weeks 2–4, research — rigor matters.** This is the one early phase where "it's just process, anything goes" is wrong. A leading interview question or a biased sample doesn't cost you a little; it corrupts everything built on top of it. Your synthesis, your How Might We statements, your final pitch all inherit whatever sloppiness went into the research. So research weeks have specific method checks, listed in each assignment.

**Weeks 5–14, ideation and prototyping — quantity wins.** You don't need every idea or every prototype to be good. You need one good one, and the bad ones cost almost nothing. This is where trying things fast and failing in public is the correct strategy, not a lack of rigor. Graded on completion.

**Weeks 15–16, the final — quality matters again.** One broken piece drags the whole thing down. Graded against a rubric you'll have well in advance.

Rigorous at the start, rough and frequent in the middle, tight and evidenced at the end.

### What every weekly submission needs

Posted to Canvas before the next class session. Same four things every time, so it becomes a habit rather than a new format to learn:

1. **Photo or screenshot** of what you made. Presence only — never judged on how it looks.
2. **Three sentences minimum**, answering: What did you build or test? What did it teach you? What's still unanswered?
3. **Reading → Change**, on weeks with an assigned reading. Name one reading. Name one specific thing you changed in your project because of it. Not "it made me think differently" — an actual change.
4. **On time.**

Each missing item is a specific, nameable reason for losing a point. Nothing else affects the grade. If you ever dispute a grade, the answer will be "component X wasn't there," never an argument about whether your work was good enough.

### Weights

| Category | Weeks | Weight |
|---|---|---|
| Design thinking & critique | 1 | 5% |
| Research & fieldwork | 2, 3, 4 | 20% |
| Synthesis & ideation | 5, 6, 7 | 15% |
| Prototyping & iteration | 9, 10, 12, 14 | 15% |
| Feedback & testing | 11, 13 | 15% |
| Final communication | 15, 16 | 30% |

Research and the final presentation carry the most weight, for the reasons above.

**70% of your grade is completion-based** — it depends on whether you did the work and posted it, not on whether it turned out well. **30% is the final**, which is the one place quality is judged, against a rubric you'll have weeks in advance.

That split is deliberate. If most of the grade rode on the final, the rational move in November would be to protect a safe idea instead of testing a risky one — which is exactly the behavior this course is built to prevent.

### The final project rubric

You get this before Week 14, not after. It's the same structure taught that week, so it should feel like an echo of the lecture rather than a surprise. Scored out of 20.

| Criterion | 1 pt | 2 pts | 3 pts | 4 pts |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **HMW & research lead-in** | Neither present | One present, not the other | Both present | Both present, and the HMW traces to specific cited research |
| **Process & evidence shown** | 0–1 iterations shown | 2 iterations | 3 iterations | 4+ iterations, each with a photo or artifact |
| **Systemic framing** | No system-level connections named | 1 named | 2 named | 3+ named, each tied to a specific design choice |
| **Reflection & open questions** | None named | 1 named | 2 named | 3+ named, each with a next step to test it |
| **Clarity & cut** | Over time, or narrates every artifact | Over time *or* over-narrates | Within 10 minutes, hits the required structure | All of that, and a stranger could restate your argument in one sentence |

Four of these five are counts — two graders would never disagree. The last one requires judgment, and I'd rather say so than pretend the whole rubric is objective when 80% of it is.

### AI tools

You may use AI tools in this course. Two rules.

**Disclose it.** Any week you used an AI tool for something you submitted, say so in your post — one line, what you used and what for. No need to make it feel like a confession, just a note.

**Never put research data into one.** No interview recordings, no transcripts, no fieldnotes that name a person. Ever. We told the Hilton their staff would stay anonymous, and a housekeeper who tells you something candid about their job is trusting that promise. Uploading that recording to a commercial service breaks it, whatever your intent.

This second rule is not an academic integrity policy. It's the same promise you make to someone before you ask them a question, and it holds for the whole semester.

We'll read and discuss what AI is doing to design practice in Week 1. It's a subject of study here as well as a tool.

MICA's institutional AI policy is included in the Policies section below.

### What This Course Actually Teaches: Six Core Skills

The CLOs list outcomes; this is the same thing restated as six teachable verbs, each with the named frameworks (beyond what's already threaded into specific weeks) worth knowing exist — not necessarily assigning all of them, but knowing the field so you can pick.

**1. Design Think** — critiquing and situating the process itself, not just executing it. Covered directly in Wk1. Other named process models worth showing students exist (there is no one true process — that's the point of Wk1): the Double Diamond (Discover/Define/Develop/Deliver — UK Design Council), the Stanford d.school 5-stage (Empathize/Define/Ideate/Prototype/Test), IDEO's Inspiration/Ideation/Implementation. Showing 3–4 competing named models in five minutes does more to make the Wk1 critique land than any single one of them on its own.

**2. Design Research** — LLAT / IDEO Method Cards (Wk1–3). Also worth knowing: LUMA Institute's *Looking / Understanding / Making* — a direct cousin of LLAT, 36 methods across a 3-phase structure rather than 4; useful as a comparison if LLAT ever feels too coarse for a given brief. Peterson & Olson's "multidimensioning" (already in the ethics reading list) is the more academic version of the same idea.

**3. Design Prototype** — the full toolkit list above (cardboard, sacrificial prototyping, Wizard-of-Oz, bodystorming). Also worth knowing: the Lean Startup *Build–Measure–Learn* loop (Eric Ries) — pairs prototyping directly to a testing/learning cycle, useful vocabulary for Wk9–13's repeated "what did this teach you, what's still unanswered."

**4. Collaborate** — currently the most *practiced* but least explicitly *taught* skill in the plan. Team research (Wk1–6) and desk crits (Wk9–11) assume students already know how to collaborate and give/receive critique well. Worth naming directly rather than assuming: art-school crit culture itself as a taught skill (a short "how to give and receive a crit" primer before Wk9's desk crits start, not just modeled implicitly), and a named lightweight decision method for splitting/merging team research (dot voting is already in the toolkit; fist-to-five consensus checks are another option) so "divide and delegate observations" (Wk1's LLAT framing) has an actual mechanic behind it.

**5. Iterate** — the strong-link/bias-toward-action argument (Wk6, Wk9) covers *why* fast, frequent, public failure is correct strategy. For *how*: the OODA loop (Observe–Orient–Decide–Act — originally military strategy, widely adapted in design/product for fast cycles) and the same Build–Measure–Learn loop from #3 above are both compact, teachable models for structuring an iteration cycle rather than just "make more stuff."

**6. Co-design** — the actual gap. As currently drafted, this course is closer to classic HCD (research *about* people, design team synthesizes and decides) than PAR/co-design (design *with* people). Sanders & Stappers' "Co-creation and the New Landscapes of Design" (already assigned Wk6) has a well-known diagram plotting exactly this spectrum — user-centered → participatory → co-design — worth actually showing in class, not just assigning as text. The PAR readings already sit in the Research Ethics section (Whyte, Carr & Kemmis), but nothing in the weekly schedule currently puts a Hilton stakeholder *inside* an ideation or prototyping session — Wk11's stakeholder visit is feedback/crit, which is still research-adjacent, not co-design. Worth deciding deliberately whether that's fine for this semester's scope, or whether one session (Wk6 ideation, most naturally) should genuinely include a staff or guest participant as a collaborator rather than a subject.

## About the Site Partner

### Site Partner: Hilton Baltimore Inner Harbor

This semester's fieldwork is grounded in a partnership with the [Hilton Baltimore Inner Harbor](https://www.hilton.com/en/hotels/bwicchh-hilton-baltimore/) (401 West Pratt Street, Baltimore, Maryland, 21201). Students conduct on-site fieldwork — observation, semi-structured interviews with staff and guests — across the property, then move through synthesis, ideation, and prototyping toward a design intervention grounded in that research. Real stakeholders (staff, management, guests, community partners) are the audience for interim and final feedback where access allows.

**The assignment, in one breath:** You've been hired by the City of Baltimore to reimagine some aspect of the Hilton Baltimore Inner Harbor — a hotel the city itself built, owns, and has subsidized by more than $140 million since it opened in 2008 to prop up the convention center next door. Your design team has to research the constituents who actually use the site — front-desk and housekeeping staff, conventioneers, leisure guests, the surrounding neighborhood, the taxpayers footing the bill whether they ever walk in or not — to decipher the interventions most worth making. The eight briefs below are entry points into that same assignment, not eight separate projects.

**Example project briefs** (all sited within the Hilton property):
- **Arrival & front desk.** The first five minutes of a stay set the tone for everything after. How might a design intervention change how guests experience arrival, check-in, or the transition from street to lobby?
- **Meeting & convention spaces.** Business travelers move through the property differently than leisure guests — different pace, different needs, different stress points. How might a design intervention improve that experience for organizers, attendees, or staff supporting an event?
- **Back-of-house & housekeeping.** The systems guests never see — staff workflows, supply logistics, turnover timing — shape the guest experience indirectly but powerfully. How might a design intervention support the people who make the property run?
- **Lobby, bar & F&B as social space.** Hotels are one of the few semi-public "third places" left in a lot of downtowns. How might a design intervention make the Hilton's public spaces more useful or engaging to guests, staff, or the surrounding neighborhood?
- **Wayfinding across a large, complex property.** Convention hotels are effectively small cities — ballrooms, towers, skybridges, parking. How might a design intervention help people (guests, first-time visitors, staff) navigate it?
- **Sustainability & resource systems.** Linen and towel reuse programs, food waste, energy use, single-use amenities. How might a design intervention make an existing sustainability effort more visible, more effective, or more participatory?
- **Civic asset & public accountability.** The Hilton Baltimore Inner Harbor is city-owned — Baltimore borrowed $301M to build it in 2005, and taxpayers have covered over $140M in operating losses since it opened in 2008, including a first-ever hotel worker strike in 2024 that preceded a new labor contract. How might a design intervention make that civic relationship — city, taxpayers, guests, workers — more visible, more legible, or more accountable to the public actually funding it?
- **Community partnership & connection.** Rodney specifically named this as a priority for the site partnership — how does the Hilton better connect with the surrounding neighborhood and community partners, not just its guests? This could include underused spaces the Hilton has already flagged as opportunities (the green roof, the outdoor restaurant space, streetscape engagement) as vectors for that connection, rather than treating them as separate asks. Especially suited to graduate students looking for a more complex engagement track.

What makes a good brief: a lot of different kinds of people to interview, many different problems or opportunities with many different goals. The point of the research is to find an emergent solution, not to arrive with one. The focus stays on how a design choice ripples through the larger system (staff labor, guest experience, resource use) rather than on an isolated artifact.

### Research Ethics & Community Engagement

Before interviews start in Wk2, spend real time on *how* to engage the people at the Hilton site — staff, guests, and any surrounding neighborhood community — respectfully, not just on how to extract insights from them. This is community building too, just aimed outward at the site rather than inward at the cohort.

- **Positionality & power.** Students are outsiders entering someone else's workplace. Front-line and housekeeping staff being interviewed by unpaid student researchers attached to a hotel-greenlit project is a real power imbalance — name it directly before anyone goes out. Guests are paying customers on their own time, not test subjects.
- **Consent, low-friction.** No IRB for a studio course, but model the practice anyway: a plain-language check-in before recording or quoting anyone ("we're design students, this is for a class project, is it okay if I ask a few questions / record this?"). Always give people an easy, comfortable "no."
- **Confidentiality for staff, specifically.** Comments about the workplace should never be traceable back to a named staff member or shift in shared class materials — a candid answer shouldn't be able to cost someone their job. Aggregate and anonymize by default.
- **Guest privacy.** No photographing guests without asking; no interrupting someone mid check-in or mid-meal. Professionalism here is a design skill, not just courtesy.
- **Safety norms.** Interview in pairs/groups; carry visible student ID; students are guests of the building, not staff, and should follow Hilton's own guest-facing conduct expectations.
- **Reciprocity.** What does a participant get out of talking to you? At minimum a genuine thank-you — ideally, real project outcomes shared back with the site, not just data extraction. Consider whether Hilton stakeholders see a plain-language findings summary before the final pitch, not only after.

**Working with humans, practically** — the section above covers *whether* to engage respectfully; this is *how*, in the actual moment, out in the field:
- **Approaching strangers.** A short, low-pressure opener beats a full project explanation — "Hi, we're design students working on a project about the hotel, could I ask you two quick questions?" Have students practice saying the opener out loud before they go out, not just write interview questions.
- **Reading the room, exiting gracefully.** Watch for disengagement (short answers, checking a phone, stepping back) and end the conversation before being asked to. "No" or "not right now" is a complete, useful answer — say thanks and move on, don't push.
- **The Mom Test principle.** Don't ask people to predict their own future behavior or validate an idea directly ("would you use X?") — ask about specific things they've actually done. Applies directly to talking with Hilton guests/staff about a proposed intervention.
- **Professionalism as a skill, not a formality.** Dress and conduct appropriate to a working hotel, not a studio — students are guests of the building twice over, once through the school's partnership and once physically.
- **De-escalation.** If someone is annoyed, confused, or suspicious about being observed or questioned — understandably, a stranger with a notebook near a workplace or hotel room can read as surveillance — the move is to identify yourself, explain briefly, and disengage if asked, not to keep explaining until they're convinced it's fine.
- **Safety as baseline.** Stay in visible pairs/groups, know where the rest of the group is, trust instinct over the research plan.

**Readings** (already sourced from the Are.na reading list):
- Madison, *Critical Ethnography: Method, Ethics, and Performance* — excerpt theory/method chapters. Bridges critical theory and fieldwork technique; connects queer, feminist, and critical race theory to the practical work of doing ethnography respectfully.
- Whyte (ed.), *Participatory Action Research* — excerpt the framing chapter. Sets up the PAR tradition: community, co-design, researcher positionality.
- Piepzna-Samarasinha, *Care Work* — excerpt the mutual-aid/collective-care chapters. A disability-justice lens on what "respectful" actually requires in practice.
- Costanza-Chock, *Design Justice*, ch. 1–2 — already assigned Wk12; worth referencing here too, since "whose needs does this design center" is really a Wk2 question as much as a Wk12 one.
- Wolcott, *The Art of Fieldwork* — assign in full or substantial excerpt. Accessible and reflective; good for students new to ethnographic thinking generally, not just the critical-theory end of it.
- Ladner, *Practical Ethnography* — step-by-step for corporate/organizational settings where timelines are short and sponsors expect actionable outcomes. The boots-on-the-ground companion to the heavier methods texts, and close to the actual Hilton situation.
- Fitzpatrick, *The Mom Test* — ruthlessly practical guide to getting honest information from people without leading them.

**Placement:** fold into the very start of Wk2, ~15–20 minutes, before the first site visit — students should have this before they ever set foot on-site, not just before they start interviewing.

## Weekly Schedule

### Wk1 — Design Thinking: Pros, Cons, and Other Ways to Frame It

**Opening thesis — put this on the first slide, before anything else:**

Herbert Simon, *The Sciences of the Artificial* (1969): "Everyone designs who devises courses of action aimed at changing existing situations into preferred ones."

One sentence, serious enough to survive this week's own critique (it predates "Design Thinking" as a brand by decades and belongs to no consultancy), and generative enough to produce the entire semester without further justification:

- **Existing situations** have to be understood before they can be changed → **Research** — you can't devise a course of action toward a situation you haven't first understood.
- **Preferred** is never neutral — preferred by whom, decided by whom — → **Think** (this is exactly where this week's critique lives: "preferred" is the load-bearing, contestable word in Simon's sentence) and **Co-design** (without the people who'll actually live in the "preferred" situation involved in defining it, you're guessing at their preference, not designing toward it).
- **Devising courses of action** means proposing something, not just describing the problem → **Prototype** — a devised course of action has to become tangible enough to evaluate.
- No single devised course of action gets it right the first time, and "changing" is a process, not an event → **Iterate**.
- At any real complexity — a hotel is hundreds of existing situations, held by hundreds of people with hundreds of different "preferred"s — no one person can hold the whole picture alone → **Collaborate**.

Come back to this sentence at the start of each new phase of the semester: "we're still just doing what Simon said — right now we're on the [research / think / prototype / iterate / collaborate / co-design] part of that sentence." One throughline for sixteen weeks instead of six disconnected units.

- Syllabus, basic intro, professor brings a brief to go over
- Ethnography introduction
- **In-class exercise — "Arrival, in miniature" (25–30 min):** stays entirely in Dolphin, no site visit needed yet.
  1. *Recall (5–8 min).* Individually, jot down: what did you notice or feel walking into Dolphin today? Where did you hesitate, get confused, or have to guess? What wasn't clear until someone told you?
  2. *Insight (5 min).* Pairs compare notes, find one specific, shared pattern, turn it into a sentence: "People hesitate at X because Y."
  3. *Prototype (10 min).* Paper, tape, marker — a fast, ugly fix. Could be literal (a sign, a floor arrow) or not.
  4. *Share (5 min).* Quick pin-up, one sentence each.

  Since it's Day 1, every student just had a real, unstaged arrival experience — no need to travel to the Hilton or stage foot traffic to get authentic material. This directly rehearses the "Arrival & front desk" project brief they'll tackle for real at the Hilton in Wk2+. It also quietly seeds two things that get named explicitly later: that research here is a team sport (no one person can hold the whole picture — comparing notes in pairs is required, not optional), and that a researcher is sometimes the observer and sometimes the observed/subject, which becomes a real question again at the Wk2 ethics briefing. Don't name "sacrificial prototype" or "strong-link" yet — those terms arrive formally in Wk4 and Wk6; let Day 1 just model the practice, then call back to it when the vocabulary shows up.

**Part 1 — Where it came from (the pro case, briefly)**
- 15-minute lecture: brief history of the Design Methods movement (1960s–70s) through Herbert Simon's foundational framing of design as a discipline, to the 2000s rise of the design consultancy (IDEO, Stanford d.school) that packaged "Design Thinking" into a named, sellable process
- Watch: Tim Brown, TED Talk — "Designers — Think Big" (the executive pitch, 17 min). This is the version of the story companies buy.
- Reference: IDEO.org Design Kit videos, Stanford d.school "How We Work" — polished, branded, institutional framing of design thinking as product

**Part 2 — The critique**
- Watch/discuss: Natasha Jen, "Design Thinking is Bullsh*t" — the most widely circulated version of this argument. Also available as write-ups in Core77, It's Nice That, and Design Week, if print excerpts work better than video for a given section.
- Watch: Jenny Wen (Design Lead, Anthropic; former Director of Design, Figma), "Why Designers Can No Longer Trust the Design Process" (Hatch Conference) — a 2026-dated version of the same critique, aimed at how the standard sequence (research → personas → journey maps → problem statement → solutions) doesn't match how design work actually happens now, especially with AI in the loop.
- Reading: Carly Ayres, "Designers! Designers! Designers!" — pair directly with Wen rather than filing separately. Wen argues the *packaged* process doesn't survive contact with AI; Ayres argues why the *underlying skills* matter more because of it — AI collapses the cost of generating artifacts, but doesn't touch research, problem framing, or translating complexity into trusted experience (Ayres' "Super IC" who owns an outcome end-to-end). Same move as the rest of Wk1: critique the branded product, not the underlying capability.
- Watch: Erika Hall, "As Little Research As Possible" (Vimeo) — a practitioner's critique of research-as-theater within the Design Thinking process.
- Reading: Blauvelt, "Design's Ethnographic Turn" (Design Observer, 2007) — assign in full. Traces how ethnography specifically got absorbed and repackaged by design consultancies. The AIGA ethnography primer he critiques was produced with Cheskin, a consumer research firm whose founder created the Marlboro Man — worth telling students that history directly.
- Reading (short): Tonkinwise, "Just Design" (Medium) — argues that prefixing "Design" onto everything (Design Thinking, Design Fiction, Speculative Design, Critical Design...) is often redundant or appropriative. A sharper, more academic cousin of Jen's argument.
- **One-stop option:** "Is Design Thinking Really BS?" (uxdesign.cc) — a virtual debate between Natasha Jen and Marty Neumeier, literally structured as pro/con in one document. Could stand in for several of the above if you want to trim the reading load rather than expand it.

**Part 3 — Other ways to frame the same underlying practice**
Not every critique of "Design Thinking" (capital D, capital T, the branded process) is a critique of design reasoning itself. Put a few alternate lenses in front of students so they don't conflate the commercial product with the underlying cognitive activity:
- Cross, "Designerly Ways of Knowing" (*Design Studies*, 1982) — design as its own epistemology, distinct from the humanities and the sciences, not a knockoff of either
- Kolko, "Abductive Thinking and Abductive Reasoning" — design reasoning as abduction (Peirce), different from the inductive/deductive reasoning students already know from other disciplines
- Celine Nguyen, "research as leisure activity" — reframes research as an ongoing curious disposition (always looking, always asking, comfortable with "useless" or shallow inquiry that doesn't obviously pay off) rather than a bounded task performed once per project. **State this connection to students explicitly, don't leave it implicit:** the actual argument for ethnographic rigor (Blauvelt, Part 2) isn't to make students more academic — broader, more sustained looking and asking produces better abductive leaps and sharper intuition. Research quality isn't about credentialing the process; it's about feeding the abductive reasoning the rest of the semester depends on.
- Frayling, "Research in Art and Design" (1993) — the research *into* / *for* / *through* design distinction; gives students language they'll need later for prototypes-as-research
- "Original design thinking = Rhetorical thinking" (Ghost in the Pixel) — reframes "design thinking" through classical rhetoric rather than Silicon Valley process language; pairs directly with the design-rhetoric material the course returns to in Wk14
- Buckminster Fuller's generalist framing ("give them a tool, the use of which will lead to new ways of thinking") — design thinking as a generalist's tool, not a specialist's branded process

**Closing activity**
- Play a few rounds of Disruptus (brainstorming card game); discuss how design research (new "cards") creates inputs for design practice — a hands-on, low-stakes first taste of *structured creativity* itself, before any of the theory above gets tested against real fieldwork
- Homework: read Blauvelt and Kolko in full before Wk2; skim Tonkinwise if time allows

**Crit frame to carry through the whole session** (reuse this same frame later in the term — see the AJ&Smart callback in Wk5–6):
- Who is the audience? What is being sold?
- What gets left out?
- Where is the research, the failure, the politics?

### Wk2 — Aug 31 · **Hilton** — First Site Visit: Look

Six hours on the property. The first visit is about looking, not asking — students have never interviewed anyone, and the point today is to make them good observers before they open their mouths.

- **Service safari (morning).** Walk the property the way a guest would: arrive from the street, find the entrance, find the front desk, find a bathroom, find the convention bridge, try to buy a coffee. Document every hesitation. Requires no interviewing skill, produces real data on a quiet Monday, and it's the "Arrival, in miniature" protocol from Wk1 run at scale — same method, bigger building.
- **Timed flow counts.** Pick a spot, count people passing in ten-minute blocks, note direction and dwell time, repeat across the day. Observation has quantitative modes. **A count of zero is a measurement** — this is the direct answer to "there's nothing to observe here."
- **Visual research.** Photos, sketches, actor lists.
- **First staff interviews.** A quiet Monday is exactly when staff have twenty minutes. Pairs only.
- **Leave the diary kits.** Hand a small self-documentation kit to willing staff — prompts, a log, a camera or phone task. Collected Wk4. This is how the class sees a busy Thursday without being there.
- **Debrief on site** before heading back, while it's fresh.

*Homework:* upload all photos and fieldnotes to the shared folder; draft interview questions for Wk4.

**References & connections**
- **Required:** Spradley, *The Ethnographic Interview* — excerpt the interview-typology section. Gives students vocabulary for interview structure and intent before they go do it.
- **Required:** Portigal, *Interviewing Users* — excerpt core interviewing chapters. Honest and practical; pairs directly with Spradley.
- **Skim:** Cross, "Designerly Ways of Knowing" (*Design Studies*, 1982). Establishes design as a discipline with its own epistemology; the theoretical ground under the rest of the term.

### Wk3 — Sep 7 · **No class** — Independent Fieldwork

Labor Day. No class session, but there is work.

- **Required: visit the property in pairs on a busy weekday.** Students arrange it themselves within the week. Mondays are the hotel's quietest day, so class visits alone will never show them a check-in rush, a convention crowd, or a queue. This is the only way they see the site under load.
- Transit covered, same as class visits. Pairs, never alone.
- *Homework:* fieldnotes and photos posted before Wk4, with one sentence naming what was different from the Monday visit.

*Instructor note: this week does the heaviest lifting in the whole research phase, and it costs nothing. The gap between a dead Monday and a busy Wednesday is itself the finding.*

### Wk4 — Sep 14 · **Hilton** — Second Site Visit: Ask

Return with better questions. The gap between Wk2 and now is where students learn what a good question is.

- **Targeted interviews** aimed at the specific gaps Wk2 and the independent visit exposed — not a repeat of the first pass.
- **Service blueprint, built standing in the building.** Map front-stage (guest-visible) and back-stage (staff-only) actions together. A hotel is the canonical case for this method, and it should be drawn where students can walk the back-stage line, not from memory in a classroom.
- **Collect the diary kits** and talk through them with the staff who filled them.
- **Shadowing, if access allows.** Thirty minutes following a front-desk or housekeeping staff member through actual work. `[confirm with partner]`
- *Homework:* transcribe and prepare interview material for coding.

### Wk5 — Sep 21 · Campus — Coding & Synthesis

- What is coding? Tag short snippets from interviews/observations with simple labels ("lost signage," "rush hour stress," "asks strangers")
- Focus on behaviors, breakdowns, workarounds, emotional moments — evidence, not opinion
- Visualize discoveries through sorting/grouping: journey maps, affinity maps, personas, 2×2 grids
- In-class: demo coding a sample quote; students code their own transcripts; swap/compare in pairs; cluster on the wall; draft quick visual summaries
- Homework: bring codes, updated visuals, short reflection

**References & connections**
- **Required:** Emerson, Fretz & Shaw, *Writing Ethnographic Fieldnotes* — excerpt the observation/thick-description chapters. The most practically useful fieldwork text; does real work alongside the Hilton site visits.
- **Skim:** Charmaz, *Constructing Grounded Theory* — excerpt the coding chapters. More accessible than Glaser/Strauss; directly teachable alongside fieldwork.
- **Precedent for the 2×2 grid:** K-HOLE's "Brand Anxiety Matrix" — a real, published 2×2 diagnostic framework from a working strategy studio. Worth showing as "here's a 2×2 built by professionals," not just a classroom exercise.
- Tool references: NN/g Journey Mapping 101, Atlassian Affinity Mapping, Harvard Library Qualitative Coding, Stanford d.school Bootcamp Bootleg, Miro templates.

### Wk6 — Sep 28 · Campus — Sacrificial Prototypes, Systems Thinking & How Might We

*The densest day of the semester. Morning is making, afternoon is framing — and that order matters: you build a rough thing, then map the system it lands in.*

**Morning — sacrificial prototyping**
- Talk through last week's visualizations. What is "insightful"?
- Sacrificial prototype: deliberately rough, incomplete, throwaway, built early to make an idea tangible enough to test, critique, or challenge — without investing time in refinement. The goal is to learn, not impress.
- In-class: add newest research to the structured data; update visualizations; write rough-draft insights

**Afternoon — systems thinking and How Might We**
- Systems-based framing: map the ecosystem (people, places, flows, forces); spot connections; the design is an intervention in a living system, not just a fix
- **Short lecture: service design.** Shostack's service blueprint (1984), front-stage and back-stage, the service moment. The discipline most directly matched to this site, and it retroactively organizes everything students saw in Wk2 and Wk4.
- Turn insights into How Might We questions; instruction on writing HMWs
- In-class: convert insights → design opportunity statements → HMWs in small groups
- *Homework:* write three good HMWs for Wk7

**References & connections**
- **Required:** Buchanan, "Wicked Problems in Design Thinking" (*Design Issues*, 1992). Essential, and directly under the wicked-problems framing in this week.
- **Skim:** Dorst, *Frame Innovation* — excerpt the introduction. Best short text on design reasoning at a high level.
- **Skim:** Frayling, "Research in Art and Design" (1993) — very short. The research *into* / *for* / *through* design distinction; language for what students are doing when a rough prototype *is* the research.
- **Warm-up:** Chindōgu — the Japanese practice of deliberately "unuseless" inventions. A quick, funny way into the sacrificial-prototype idea: it's allowed to be absurd, because the point is what you learn, not the object.
- **Framing quote:** Buckminster Fuller — "If you want to teach people a new way of thinking, don't bother trying to teach them. Instead, give them a tool, the use of which will lead to new ways of thinking."
- **Shelf:** Fuller, "Specialized in Generality" (BFI) — connects to the course's goal of students identifying as generalists rather than by co-major.

### Wk7 — Oct 5 · **Hilton** — Ideation On Site

*This is the co-design session. Everything before it researched people; this one designs with them.*

- **Show the Sanders & Stappers spectrum diagram first** — user-centered → participatory → co-design — and tell students what's about to happen to them. Naming the shift is the lesson.
- Edit/select insights; pick one good HMW from each student to reach 12–18
- Brainstorm across most of the HMWs; categorize, dedupe
- **Hilton staff join part of the session as participants, not subjects.** A quiet Monday and their own building is the only circumstance where this is easy — they'd never travel to campus for it.
- **Bodystorming in the real space.** Act out a proposed flow in the actual lobby, with students playing guest and staff. Useless in a classroom, obvious here.
- HOMES/POEMS: human, object, message, environment, service — as a checklist across fieldwork, synthesis, ideation
- Push on "why": keep focus on services and systems — the artifact is a way to access the service/system
- *Homework:* reflect on the idea post-sorting — what might be the positive and negative effects. **Name one decision your team disagreed on and how you resolved it.**

**References & connections**
- **Required:** Sanders & Stappers, "Co-creation and the New Landscapes of Design" (*CoDesign*, 2008) — short enough to assign in full. Show the diagram in class, don't just assign the text.
- **Required (short):** Adam Mastroianni, "Science is a Strong-Link Problem." You don't need every HMW to be good, you need *one* to be good, and the weak ones cost almost nothing. This is the argument for why generating a big pile of mediocre ideas on purpose is correct strategy, not a failure to be efficient.

### Wk8 — Oct 12 · **No class** — Fall Break

### Wk9 — Oct 19 · Campus — Low-Fidelity Prototyping; Individual Work Begins

*The pivot. Teams dissolve; each student takes one prompt forward alone — but the work still comes back to the room every week.*

- **Short primer: how to give and receive a crit.** Named directly rather than assumed. Desk crits start next week and run to the end of term; this is a taught skill, not a formality.
- Storyboards, paper/foam mockups, diagrams
- Prototyping demo: paper mockups as stand-ins, foam/balsa for quick mockups
- Keep focus on services and systems, not the artifact in isolation
- In-class: discuss possible effects and implications of the chosen concepts
- **Plain-language findings summary goes to the Hilton this week**, so the partner sees the direction while it can still change
- *Homework:* rough prototypes on individual projects

**References & connections**
- **Demo material:** Instructables, "11 Ways to Cut and Connect Cardboard" (handout); "How to Make a Cardboard Prototype" (screen in class); balsa/paper iPhone mockup by Carolyn Li-Madeo.
- **Skim:** Sanders & Stappers, *Convivial Toolbox* — excerpt front-end research and participatory methods sections.
- **Shelf:** Holtzblatt & Beyer, *Contextual Design* — excerpt the model chapters. Structured framework for moving from fieldwork to synthesis.

### Wk10 — Oct 26 · Campus — Increasing Fidelity, Desk Crits, Visiting Designer

- **A visiting designer runs small desk crits alongside the class's own.**
- Faculty floats and helps move students along
- Increasing fidelity means real iterative improvement (quality, scale, materials) — but can also mean an increase in the knowledge level defining the next prototype, not just "I made it, I'm done"
- Can you scale to actual size? Cardboard to plywood? 3D model to 3D print? What did this prototype teach you, what's still unanswered?
- Multiple low-fidelity prototypes that build real understanding beat one high-fidelity prototype with better looks and no learning
- **Standing questions, every remaining work week:** how to make it testable and retestable; who do you test with first, next, last; interrogate object, service, and system levels via HOMES/POEMS; who maintains or fabricates it; why would this community or this hotel get excited about it; what are the sustainability questions (materials, energy); what are the social and labor ramifications
- In-class: also discuss how to structure a messy project so a non-designer can give useful feedback without being confused — direct prep for next week
- *Homework:* bring a testable prototype to the Hilton next week

**References & connections**
- **Fidelity-doesn't-mean-leaving-materials-behind:** Chairigami's cardboard sofa, Luo Studio's cardboard exhibition space — cardboard at genuinely high finish, a counter to "cardboard = rough draft only."
- **Reiterate the strong-link point from Wk7:** a bad prototype costs almost nothing, so make fast and often, fail in the open, and let the failure move the work. Restate it here, mid-project, when the temptation to protect one "good" prototype is highest.
- **Optional site connection:** invite a Hilton stakeholder to join this crit alongside the visiting designer — a first real-audience reaction before next week's formal testing.

### Wk11 — Nov 2 · **Hilton** — Test With Real Users

- How do you collect feedback, structure it, and decide how much explanation an external tester needs?
- Validation: what are you trying to prove or disprove?
- **Test on the property** — interviews, actual user tests, storyboard walkthroughs, bodystorming with real staff and guests
- *Homework:* what broke, and what will you change before you go back

**References & connections**
- **Required:** Costanza-Chock, *Design Justice*, ch. 1–2 — excerpt. Whose needs is this design actually centering, and does the feedback method reflect that? Read it before going back to real people, not after.

### Wk12 — Nov 9 · Campus — Integrating Feedback

Work time and desk crits. What did the site testing teach you? How would you run it differently? Revise for the retest.

*Instructor note: this is the hinge of the whole feedback loop. A student who changes nothing this week has not understood what happened last week.*

*Homework:* bring a revised version to the Hilton next week.

### Wk13 — Nov 16 · **Hilton** — Retest

*The point of the second test is not more feedback. It is finding out whether the fix worked.*

- Retest the revised prototype with staff and guests — ideally some of the same people
- Did the change solve what it was supposed to solve? Did it create something new?
- One test is a check. Two tests are a loop. This is the week the course's iteration outcome actually gets demonstrated.
- *Homework:* bring a final-fidelity iteration to Wk14, prepared to discuss presentation plans

### Wk14 — Nov 23 · Campus — Design as Rhetoric

- Design communication as persuasive storytelling: a logical argument that convinces others of your project's systemic value
- Structure: HMW & research lead-in → why the HMW mattered → end solution → key features → ripple/systems connections → evidence from prototypes and tests
- The persuasive part isn't walking someone through every single step
- In-class: students outline their pitch using the structure above; discuss what's needed for slides (photos, storyboards, mapped connections)
- Homework: start the final presentation; finalize documentation; draft the full explanation (~10 min)

**References & connections**
- **Framing quote:** Rem Koolhaas, via Michael Rock — "You must reduce everything to its most brutal contradiction." Sharp pull-quote for exactly this week's task: deciding what to cut.

### Wk15 — Draft Presentations

One cover page/poster; keep to 10 minutes or under; help students see what needs to be presented vs. what can be omitted. In-class: review drafts, practice presenting to each other. Homework: final adjustments.

### Wk16 — Pitch Day

- Deliverables: slide deck with a clear narrative (not an exhaustive walkthrough) — what HMW was the vector, what was learned, what didn't work, what supports the conclusion, what's still open
- Homework: upload deck to Canvas; written self-reflection on agency, collaboration, and how engaging the Hilton site affected the designed artifact. Prompts: how was this process different from individual creative practice; where did collaboration positively/negatively affect the process.

**References & connections**
- **Site connection:** where access allows, invite Hilton stakeholders as part of the pitch-day audience — closes the loop on the site partnership from Wk1 fieldwork through to final delivery, and gives the "persuasive rhetoric" framing from Wk14 a real, non-classroom audience.

## Sustainability & Circularity — Lightweight Throughline

Not a dedicated unit — there isn't room in sixteen weeks for a full sustainability module — but worth touching deliberately at the points where it's already almost present, rather than leaving it as an accident.

- **Already built in as a project brief.** "Sustainability & resource systems" (linen/towel reuse, food waste, energy, single-use amenities) is already one of the eight example briefs above — some teams may pick it directly.
- **Materials circularity in prototyping (Wk4, 7, 9).** Cardboard and balsa wood are already the default prototyping materials for reasons of speed and cost — worth one explicit line noting that's also a circularity choice, not just a cheap one: reclaimed cardboard has minimal embodied energy and is genuinely recyclable or compostable at end of life, unlike foam board or 3D-printed plastic. A "sacrificial" prototype that gets thrown away matters more, and costs less to throw away, when the material itself is low-impact.
- **Already half-built into the standing questions.** Wk9–13's repeated prompt list already includes "what are the sustainability questions? materials? energy?" — the gap wasn't the content, it was naming it as a deliberate thread instead of a stray bullet buried in a longer list.
- **Further-reading pointers, not assigned:** SUX (Sustainable UX Network), Design Council's "Skills for Planet," and a piece on "Zoöp Futures" (organizational cooperation between humans and more-than-humans) — all already surfaced from the Are.na channel. Good to have on hand if a student's project heads this direction on its own; no need to build a lecture around them unless one does.

## Design Methods & Tools Toolkit

A bank to pull from — not everything here needs to be assigned to a specific week, but good to have on hand when a week needs a different exercise than what's drafted.

**Collecting (research / LLAT: Learn, Look, Ask)**
- Semi-structured interviews (Spradley, Portigal — Wk2)
- Contextual inquiry / shadowing — observe someone doing their actual job/task in situ, minimal interruption
- Diary studies — participant self-logs behavior over days/weeks; could work well with Hilton staff across shifts
- Cultural probes — leave-behind kits (disposable camera, journal, prompts) for self-documentation
- Photo elicitation — ask participants to photograph something, then talk you through their choices
- Stakeholder mapping — who touches the system, and how
- Service safari / mystery-guest walkthrough — students experience the service themselves, front to back, before interviewing anyone

**Sorting / Synthesis**
- Affinity mapping, journey mapping, personas, 2×2 grids (Wk3)
- Empathy maps — says/thinks/does/feels quadrant, a quicker persona alternative
- Service blueprints — map front-stage (guest-visible) and back-stage (staff-only) actions together; very on-the-nose for a hotel site
- Card sorting — have stakeholders group/prioritize concepts themselves rather than the design team guessing categories
- Assumption mapping — plot ideas by known/unknown × important/unimportant to target the riskiest assumptions first

**Brainstorming / Ideation**
- How Might We (Wk5) — sourced from AJ&Smart, a Berlin-based studio that literally sells design-thinking/design-sprint workshops for a living. A few of their other fast facilitation formats worth knowing beyond the one already linked:
  - **Crazy 8s** — 8 ideas in 8 minutes (fold a page into 8 panels, one idea per panel, done fast on purpose). Strong companion to Wk6 ideation.
  - **Lightning Decision Jam (LDJ)** — problem → reframe → idea → prioritize → prototype, compressed into one facilitated session. Could be a useful structure for the Wk6 in-class ideation flow specifically.
  - **Note-and-Vote / dot voting** — silent idea generation on sticky notes, then anonymous dot voting to prioritize; avoids loudest-voice-in-the-room bias.
  - (Their videos tend to end with a plug for their paid course — fine to link, just expect the pitch.)
  - **Worth naming out loud to students:** AJ&Smart is a design-thinking-workshop vendor — precisely the commercial apparatus the Wk1/Supplemental crit session interrogates (Natasha Jen, Jenny Wen). Using their techniques in Wk5–6 while critiquing the industry that produces them isn't a contradiction to paper over — it's a live example for the "who is the audience, what is being sold" crit frame. A technique can be useful and still be a product. Consider flagging this explicitly in class rather than hoping no one notices.
- Disruptus card game (Wk1)
- SCAMPER — Substitute / Combine / Adapt / Modify / Put to another use / Eliminate / Reverse; a structured prompt set for pushing past first ideas
- Worst Possible Idea — deliberately brainstorm bad ideas first to loosen up the room before a real round; pairs well with the strong-link framing already in Wk6 (explicit permission to generate junk)
- Bodystorming / role-play — physically act out a scenario in the space rather than only talking or sketching it

**Prototyping**
- Sacrificial/low-fi prototyping — cardboard, paper, foam, balsa wood (Wk4, Wk7)
- Storyboarding
- Bodystorming as prototyping — walk a proposed new service flow physically, with "guest" and "staff" roles played by students
- Wizard-of-Oz prototyping — fake the "smart" part of a system with a hidden human performing it; useful for anything staff- or tech-mediated at the Hilton site (e.g., a proposed check-in flow, a signage system)

**Testing / Feedback**
- Think-aloud usability walkthroughs
- Structured feedback forms (Wk11–12)
- Return-to-site validation interviews (Wk12)

## Supplemental Session: Critiquing the Design Thinking Gospel

Not yet slotted to a specific week — the Are.na reading-list dump stages this as its own standalone session. Consider running it either as an add-on to Wk1 or as a separate mid-semester session once students have enough process experience to critique it from the inside.

**Primary material (watch/critique together):**
- Tim Brown, TED Talk — "Designers — Think Big" (the executive pitch, 17 min)
- IDEO.org Design Kit videos — polished, branded, worth interrogating
- d.school "How We Work" content — institutional framing of design thinking as product

**Counterpoints:**
- Erika Hall, "As Little Research As Possible" (Vimeo) — honest, critical, the antidote to design-thinking hype
- Dunne & Raby — any available lecture on speculative/critical design as an alternative tradition

**Crit frame for the session:**
- Who is the audience? What is being sold?
- What gets left out?
- Where is the research, the failure, the politics?
- How does design get packaged, and for whom?
- Note for students: the AIGA ethnography primer that Blauvelt critiques was produced with Cheskin, a consumer research firm whose founder created the Marlboro Man. The packaging of ethnography has a history worth knowing.
- Same move, closer to home: AJ&Smart, whose "How Might We" and Crazy 8s techniques the class uses in Wk5–6, is itself a design-sprint-workshop vendor. Worth pointing back at this later in the term — using a tool doesn't mean endorsing the industry that packaged it, but pretending the tool has no packaging is exactly the naivety this session is arguing against.
