# DI 200 Structured Creativity

## Official Course Description:

DI 200 / Structured Creativity / 3Cr
Students will learn the tools of the human-centred design process while tackling a contemporary and relevant issue each semester. The HCD process includes site-visits and in-person interviews to gather insights, structured synthesis of those insight in to actionable opportunities for form-giving, directed and divergent brainstorm, prototyping and feedback loops for iterative refinement, and creating engaging narrative documentation to communicate novel design solutions developed from the initial insights.

## Official Course Learning Outcomes:

- Demonstrate bias-toward action prototyping and visualization skills
- Learn how to structure and participate in an effective brainstorming session
- Demonstrate design process with iterative prototyping
- Employ ethnographic research methods
- Synthesize human-centered research into actionable design prompts.
- Execute team-based design decisions.
- Deploy relevant testing and feed-back loops for iterative project development.

Main Project
This semester-long project immerses students in a human-centered design process to reimagine an aspect of a real-world public space or system, through on-site fieldwork with people from that community (e.g., talking to users, staff, or visitors, not necessarily top officials).

Essential Project Elements
Off-Site Fieldwork: Students must leave the studio to conduct hands-on research in a real-world environment (e.g., site visits, observations, or interactions at a public location like a transit hub or community space).
New Contexts: immerse students in behaviors, environments, cultural dynamics that are new or unfamiliar to them, encouraging empathy and insight (reinforcing research phases as important — designers have to be good at quickly getting up to speed on new things, and then quickly turn that basic understanding into prototypes)
Systemic Complexity:
Warrants mapping (e.g., visualizing flows, actors, and interconnections to build systems literacy).
Rewards thorough, evidence-based research (e.g. through methods to uncover nuanced insights, the class has to then teach the students a number of frameworks and methods to do this!)
Requires multiple rounds of prototyping and iteration (e.g., from low-fidelity mockups to tested refinements).
Ideally includes conflicting stakeholder objectives (e.g. diverse needs from users, staff, or communities) to add realism and challenge resolution skills.

Instructors can adapt these elements to fit their expertise — emphasizing sustainability conflicts in an environmental site, or social equity in a community setting.

A successful design intervention will be grounded in observations and insights that lead to tangible artifacts within this ecosystem. The design process must make clear these observations and provide reasoning through an iterative design process. Students should be taken clearly through an initial research phase, a prototyping phase, a reflection phase, further rounds of prototyping, and a final explanation phase where they are able to make sense of the previous phases.

The final deliverable will be 15 minute oral/visual presentations of the design interventions that includes the following: 
examples from the research that informed the final design, 
edited early concepts that informed how you, the designer, came to understand the design problem being addressed, 
refined iterations that show a testing loop, 
and a final visualization or prototype that clearly communicates design intent.  

The goals of this project are about students learning research methodologies, intellectual frameworks for problem identification, how analysis of problem identification becomes design directions, prototyping tools and skills, how to interrogate and iterate those prototypes, and ultimately how you find, frame, and explain your design direction and resulting decisions. The actual design “solutions” are less important than the holistic process involved to get there, and that students can explain the steps involved at the end. Process and progress over perfection.

Example Project Briefs:
Baltimore Penn Station. You’ve been hired by the city of Baltimore to reimagine some aspect of Penn station. Your design team has to research constituents that use Penn station — staff, visitors, commuters, community members, etc. — to decipher the most useful interventions.
Farmers Markets. Farmers Markets are vibrant sites, engaging the community in systems of local production and consumption. How might a design intervention make this system more useful or engaging? 
Inner Harbor. The Inner Harbor is a confluence of individual behaviors and civic engagements. How might a design intervention enhance the inner harbor experiences for the citizens of Baltimore? 
The Hilton Baltimore Inner Harbor. You've been hired by the City of Baltimore to reimagine some aspect of the Hilton Baltimore Inner Harbor — a hotel the city itself built, owns, and has subsidized by more than $140 million since it opened in 2008 to prop up the convention center next door. Your design team has to research the constituents who actually use the site — front-desk and housekeeping staff, conventioneers, leisure guests, the surrounding neighborhood, the taxpayers footing the bill whether they ever walk in or not — to decipher the interventions most worth making.

What makes a good example is a lot of different kinds of people to interview; many different sorts of problems or opportunities with many different goals. The point of doing the research is to find an emergent solution not to already have a solution in mind. The research generates the outcome as opposed to starting with an outcome.

The focus is on creating thoughtful solutions that consider how they impact larger systems (like the environment, human behavior, or social connections) rather than designing isolated artifacts. Students should think about the ripple effects of what they design.

Journey Maps (for time/steps) ￼
A journey map is a visual timeline of a user’s experience as they move through a process, space, or system. It highlights what happens at each stage, what the user does, feels, and encounters, and where pain points or opportunities arise.
Use for: Understanding sequences, transitions, and emotional highs/lows.
Example: Mapping a commuter’s experience from entering a train station to boarding a train, noting confusion, stress, or delight at each step.
Affinity Maps (for themes) ￼
Affinity mapping is a way to organize lots of observations, quotes, or ideas by grouping them into themes. You write each data point on a sticky note, then cluster similar notes together to reveal patterns.
Use for: Synthesizing raw data into actionable themes.
Example: After interviews, you might group notes about “signage confusion,” “helpful staff,” and “crowd navigation” to see which issues are most common.
Personas (for archetypes) ￼
A persona is a fictional, evidence-based character that represents a key user type. It’s built from real data and describes goals, behaviors, frustrations, and needs.
Use for: Humanizing research, focusing design decisions, and aligning teams.
Example: “Commuter Carla” is a persona who values speed and clarity, struggles with unclear signage, and relies on mobile apps for navigation.
2x2 Grids (for choices) ￼
A 2x2 grid is a simple chart with two axes (e.g., impact vs. effort) that helps you compare, prioritize, or categorize ideas, opportunities, or user needs.
A 2x2 grid is a flexible decision-making tool that helps teams quickly visualize and prioritize ideas based on criteria chosen for the project. In human-centered design, the axes are context-driven—what you value shapes what you focus on next.
Use for: Making strategic decisions, visualizing trade-offs, or clustering options.
Example: Plotting design ideas by “ease of implementation” and “potential benefit” to decide which to prototype first.
In human-centered design (HCD), the real power of the 2x2 grid is its adaptability: you choose axes that reflect what matters most for the challenge at hand.
It’s not just a tool for sorting ideas—it’s a way to surface what you value, collectively or individually, and to make those values visible and debatable. The grid helps you decide what to do next, what to focus on, and what to set aside. The criteria you use (the axes) should emerge from the project’s context, stakeholder needs, and the kind of impact you’re seeking.
Why use these? They help teams move from messy, qualitative data to clearer, visual arguments—making it easier to spot patterns, share insights, and decide what to do next.

Sacrificial Prototype, 
is it possible to build a question?
A sacrificial prototype is a deliberately rough, incomplete, or “throwaway” model created early in the design process. Its purpose is to make an idea tangible enough to test, critique, or challenge—without investing time or resources in refinement. The goal is to learn, not to impress. You build it fast, out of whatever’s on hand—cardboard, tape, sketches, scraps—knowing it will be questioned, poked, maybe even destroyed. This kind of prototype invites honest feedback and surfaces blind spots, because no one is afraid to break it or say what’s wrong. It’s an invitation to see failure as fuel for progress, a tool for asking better questions and finding deeper truths before any polish or pride gets in the way.

What is the insight?
Systems based!
See the bigger picture. Map the ecosystem—people, places, flows, forces. Spot how everything connects. Your design isn’t just a fix; it’s a thoughtful intervention in a living system. Ask: Where are you intervening, and why here? What ripple effects will your work create?
Zoom out: See beyond isolated problems; map the whole ecosystem—people, places, flows, forces.
Connections matter: Track how changes in one part ripple across others, sometimes in surprising ways.
Context counts: Ask not just what’s broken, but why—look for root causes, histories, and hidden patterns.
Intervention with intent: Design choices should address system-level needs, not just surface fixes.
Feedback loops: Consider how your intervention will be adopted, resisted, or evolve over time.
Why do this? Systems thinking reveals leverage points, avoids “band-aid” solutions, and supports resilient, ethical, and meaningful design.

Good/Bad HMWs
What Makes a Good “How Might We” (HMW) Question? 
Open-ended, not leading:
Invites exploration and multiple possible solutions, rather than steering toward a single answer.
Actionable and specific:
Focuses on a clear challenge, context, or user/system need—grounded in evidence from research.
Human/system-centered:
Frames the question around real people, behaviors, or system dynamics, not just technology or aesthetics.
Rooted in insights:
Emerges from actual observations, interviews, or data—not assumptions or personal opinions.
Avoids solutions in disguise:
Doesn’t sneak in a proposed fix (“How might we build an app to…”), but leaves room for creative approaches.
Examples 
Good HMWs:
How might we help commuters navigate Penn Station more confidently during rush hour?
How might we make farmers market waste streams more visible and actionable for vendors and shoppers?
How might we foster spontaneous social connections at the Inner Harbor without disrupting existing routines?
How might we support staff in adapting to changing crowd flows in public spaces?
Bad/Unhelpful HMWs:
How might we create an app for Penn Station navigation?
(Already assumes the solution is an app.)
How might we make everything perfect for everyone?
(Too broad, unrealistic, and not actionable.)
How might we fix the signage problem?
(Vague, doesn’t specify who, when, or why.)
How might we force people to recycle more?
(Implies a solution and uses negative language.)
Quick Checklist for a Good HMW 
Is it based on real research or observation?
Does it invite multiple possible solutions?
Is it focused on a specific user/system need?
Does it avoid suggesting a specific fix?
Is it clear, concise, and positive in tone?
In short:
A good HMW question opens up creative, evidence-based exploration. A bad one closes it down, prescribes a fix, or ignores the real context.
