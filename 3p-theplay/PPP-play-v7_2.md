# People Processing Plastic: The Play

---

*People come in.*
*Conversations happen.*
*Someone leaves changed.*
*The door opens again.*

---

## A Note on Production

Preferably, one would somehow perform this in a working plastic workshop. The shredder, the extruder, the press are best when not props, but when they run. They're loud, and the room smells of warm plastic, it is visceral. The tile made during the performance is real.

The Bottle and the Microplastic are documentary voices, they're really there for exposition. They state facts about their own existence. They're triggered by words in the human conversation — someone says *microplastics*, the Microplastic speaks. They never introduce themselves, they just come in, preferably as sort of *voices of god*. If you can't easily mic someone backstage and do the lines over a PA, then a person standing just offstage will do. 

All roles are written for any performer. No character need have a gender.

## Cast

**KRIS** — Founded the workshop. Believes the objects are an argument, and that where the material came from is most of the argument. Would rather send someone away with an idea than a sale.

**CHRIS** — Believes the objects are the argument, and an argument nobody buys isn't one. Wants to deliver, however they can.

**THE GUEST** — Came to buy tiles for a kitchen floor. Seventy square feet.

**THE NEW GUEST** — Arrives at the end.

**THE BOTTLE** & **THE MICROPLASTIC** — Expository Voices.

## Scene I — The Order

*A Precious Plastic workshop. Shredder, extruder warming up, sorting table. Bins labeled HDPE, PET, PP. A shelf of finished tiles — multicolored, marbled. Four dense grey-blue forms in the corner, the shape of milk jugs. On the walls: hand-made posters, resin codes, a circle diagram. Near the door: PPP — People Processing Plastic.*

*KRIS at the sorting table. CHRIS at the shredder, feeding in bottle caps — red, blue, white.*

**THE BOTTLE:** I am made from material that can last forever.

**THE BOTTLE:** But, do you know how long the average bottle lasts?

**THE BOTTLE:** Days in a truck. Weeks on a shelf.

**THE BOTTLE:** Minutes in a hand.

**THE BOTTLE:** Centuries after that.

**THE BOTTLE:** More time was spent moving me to you than you spent using me.

**THE BOTTLE:** I am PET. Fewer than half of us are collected. Of the ones collected, seven percent come back as bottles.

**THE BOTTLE:** The rest become something lesser. Or nothing.

*The shredder runs. CHRIS shuts it off. Silence.*

*CHRIS moves to the shelf, checks a stack of molds, sets them down again without really finishing.*

**CHRIS:** The café wants forty more.

**KRIS:** We already made them their twenty.

**CHRIS:** They liked the twenty. They want more.

**KRIS:** *(still sorting)* We're not a tile company.

*CHRIS drifts to the sorting table, picks up a cap, turns it over, sets it back in the wrong bin.*

**CHRIS:** We're not an anything, except maybe a performance.

*Pause.*

**KRIS:** *(still sorting)* Call it a demonstration then.

*CHRIS's hands go still.*

**CHRIS:** A demonstration nobody's paying for.

*KRIS holds up a piece of plastic. Reads the number on the bottom. Sets it into a bin. CHRIS picks up a recently dropped off bag of bottles*

**KRIS:** That bag's mixed. Sort it before it goes in.

**CHRIS:** It's mostly HDPE.

**KRIS:** The numbers on the bottles aren't based on "mostly."

*CHRIS pulls the bag. Sorts it. This exchange has clearly happened before.*

**CHRIS:** *(holding up a red cap)* Somebody dropped off a crate of these. All the same red. What are all of these? Forty years of one family's ketchup bottle caps?

**KRIS:** *(Pause, genuinely)* Huh.

**CHRIS:** Do you reckon it's enough for a tabletop? That'd be a tabletop with a good story.

*Kris starts to answer. The door opens. THE GUEST steps in.*

**THE GUEST:** Sorry — are you open? I'm looking for tiles.

*CHRIS turns around immediately, suddenly smiling. KRIS does not.*

**CHRIS:** Yes! We're open.

**THE GUEST:** I saw these sort of marbled plastic tiles at a café around the corner. Behind the espresso machine. I asked where they came from and I was told to head down the street and look for the 3P and smiling recycling symbol?

**CHRIS:** That's us.

**THE GUEST:** I want to do my kitchen floor.

*Pause. KRIS stops sorting.*

**KRIS:** How much floor?

**THE GUEST:** About seventy square feet. It's a galley. It's tiny.

*KRIS and CHRIS look at each other. Something passes between them that THE GUEST can't read. Silence.*

**CHRIS:** Seventy square feet.

**THE GUEST:** Is that a lot?

**CHRIS:** *(picking up a handful of flakes)* Depends on what you're measuring I guess? It definitely will need a lot of flakes!

*THE GUEST comes further in. Looks at the shelf. Picks up a tile. Turns it over. There's a number molded into the bottom.*

**THE GUEST:** What does this number mean? This can't just be the second tile you made?

*CHRIS starts restacking the tile shelf as they talk, not really looking at what they're doing.*

**CHRIS:** That's its Resin code *(points at resin code poster on wall)*. Every piece of plastic has one. Tells you what it's made of. That's a two. HDPE — high-density polyethylene. Anything we remake we renumber correctly.

**THE GUEST:** And the bins?

**CHRIS:** One number per bin. Keep them separate, you keep the value. Mix them—

**KRIS:** Landfill.

**THE GUEST:** Even the ones that look alike? Two and four?

**CHRIS:** HDPE, LDPE — low density version, same family. I don't sweat those two touching.

**KRIS:** Different melting points. Mix them, the batch comes out weaker than either alone.

**CHRIS:** Marginally.

**KRIS:** Still weaker.

*CHRIS abandons the tiles half-stacked, checks a mold instead.*

**CHRIS:** We try to keep the colors separate too, when they come in.

**THE GUEST:** So this tile was — what, bottles?

**CHRIS:** That one was mostly just the caps. Different colors because different flavors and brands. Same material. That's how we get the heathering and marbling. Then we mix them on purpose, for whatever looks interesting.

**THE GUEST:** Tiles out of trash. Cool.

*Pause. They've heard this word.*

**CHRIS:** We prefer *waste.* Trash is a decision. Waste is material between purposes.

**THE GUEST:** *(maybe the waste comment went over their head)* How many caps in one tile?

**CHRIS:** Depends on thickness. We can make them to preference. That one, maybe ninety.

**THE GUEST:** And seventy square feet?

*CHRIS doesn't answer immediately. KRIS answers instead.*

**KRIS:** About twenty-five thousand caps.

*THE GUEST looks at the bins. Then at the tile in their hand.*

**THE GUEST:** How many do you have?

**KRIS:** Today? Maybe two thousand. Assorted colors.

**THE GUEST:** So when would you have enough to make a kitchen's worth of tiles?

**KRIS:** Depends what people bring us. Or how particular for a color or pattern you are.

**THE GUEST:** That's not an answer.

**KRIS:** No, not really… but it's the way this place works.

**CHRIS:** *(under their breath)* Or doesn't quite work…

*THE GUEST moves further in, uncertain now. Stops at the corner — four solid forms, whitish grey-blue, marbled. Crouches. Lifts one. Surprised at heft.*

**THE GUEST:** What are these?

**CHRIS:** Milk jugs.

**THE GUEST:** They're solid.

**CHRIS:** Yeah. Milk jugs MADE of solid milk jugs.

**THE GUEST:** How many?

*CHRIS crosses to the corner, taps each of the other three jugs in turn as he counts.*

**CHRIS:** Roughly fifty-five. Those four together, two hundred and twenty. People are always bringing them in. Needed something to do with the excess.

**THE GUEST:** How much does it weigh?

**CHRIS:** About the same as a milk-filled one.

*THE GUEST stands. Looks at it differently.*

*Pause.*

**THE GUEST:** So can you make my floor tiles or not?

*KRIS and CHRIS look at each other again. Silence.*

**CHRIS:** Yes.

**KRIS:** No.

*Lights shift.*

## Scene II — The Misclassification

*Nobody moves. THE GUEST looks between them.*

**THE GUEST:** Is that a joke?

**CHRIS:** No.

*KRIS breaks first, goes back to the sorting table. CHRIS doesn't move.*

**CHRIS:** *(to KRIS)* Twenty-five thousand caps at forty a week is—

**KRIS:** Twelve years.

**CHRIS:** If we put the word out we can do it faster. The ketchup caps crate came in off one flyer last week… though those people seemingly collected them for 40 years… yikes.

**KRIS:** And then we're a floor company with a waiting list.

**CHRIS:** Yeah! We'd be a company. 

*THE GUEST, awkward, still holding the tile.*

**THE GUEST:** I could just — I mean, I could drive out to Floor & Decor and buy tile?

**KRIS:** You could. What would you get?

**THE GUEST:** I don't know. Whatever's on sale.

**KRIS:** That would be some kind of vinyl probably. Four dollars a square foot. Three hundred bucks, delivered in maybe two business days.

*Pause.*

**KRIS:** You know what vinyl plank is?

**THE GUEST:** …Vinyl?

**KRIS:** Right, yeah, it's short for PVC. Polyvinylchloride. Resin code three. You'd be buying a plastic floor. But brand new plastic, still crude oil only a couple of months ago. And it's one of the hardest plastics there is to recover — almost none of it ever gets recycled.

**THE GUEST:** So I'd be buying a plastic floor either way.

*KRIS goes back to sorting. Doesn't answer. Silence.*

**THE GUEST:** Let me ask you something related? I recycle. Properly. Everything gets rinsed. Everything goes in the same bin. Why isn't that enough?

*KRIS stops sorting.*

**KRIS:** Everything? What bin do the caps go in?

**THE GUEST:** Same one. Everything goes in the same one.

**KRIS:** Single stream. One truck, one bin, sorted later.

**THE GUEST:** Right.

**KRIS:** *(holding up a red cap)* This doesn't usually survive that. It's too small. 

*He spreads the fingers of his other hand into a rough grid, lets the cap drop through onto the table. Picks it back up.*

**KRIS:** At the facility it drops through the screens with the glass shards and the grit and goes out as residue.

*Silence.*

**THE GUEST:** The caps.

**CHRIS:** All of them. Every cap you've ever rinsed. I mean, unless you left it screwed on the bottle… which is its own separate set of problems since usually a cap is HDPE and the bottles they're on are PET… They have different densities so you can… I digress.

**KRIS:** *(still turning the cap over)* And the glass shatters on the way in — I mean, that's partially how the line separates it — but now there's broken glass everywhere. In the paper bales. In the plastic. Abrasive contamination drives maintenance costs up sharply, everything is basically being sand-blasted.

**THE GUEST:** So why do it that way?

**KRIS:** Because nobody who designed it had to sort it. *(then, flatly)* One bin's cheaper to collect than three, four, or ten. More people “participate,” a single, simple truck goes down the alley, labour costs drop. It's an excellent collection system.

**THE GUEST:** But not a recovery system.

**KRIS:** Yeah, you got it. It was optimized for ease of collection. Not for actually doing much with the materials afterward.

*THE GUEST sets down the tile. Takes a few steps deeper into the shop. Picks up a bottle from the nearest bin, turns it over, checks the number. Neither of them comments, but both notice.*

**THE GUEST:** And plastics that don't get sorted?

**CHRIS:** They disperse. And then get small.

**THE GUEST:** Small?

**KRIS:** Plastic doesn't really "decompose."" It breaks down. Into pieces too small to sort, too small to collect, too small to see. But, still the same plastic polymers, and worse, still permanent. Microplastic.

*Silence.*

**THE MICROPLASTIC:** I am a plastic fragment under five millimeters.

**THE MICROPLASTIC:** I am being made right now. By the polyester shirt on your back. Polyester is PET — polyethylene terephthalate. Resin code one. The same polymer as a water bottle.

**THE MICROPLASTIC:** One load of polyester releases half a million fibers. Polyester is fifty-nine percent of all fiber made on earth.

**THE MICROPLASTIC:** I am also every polyethelene thank you bag that went brittle in the sun. Every length of polypropylene rope that frayed in the water. 

**THE MICROPLASTIC:** I am what happens to plastic when it's thrown away. I am what happens while plastic is being used.

**THE MICROPLASTIC:** I have been found in human blood. In lungs. In breast milk. Particles under ten micrometers cross the placental barrier. I was in your body before your body was born.

*Long silence.*

**THE GUEST:** So a floor made of this — that's plastic that isn't just going to be out lose into the world.

**KRIS:** *(carefully)* Yes.

**CHRIS:** *(to KRIS)* Say the rest of it.

**KRIS:** Seventy square feet of tile is twenty-five thousand caps that stay solid instead. It is definitely a reliable way to sort of "sequester" plastic.

**CHRIS:** *(seemingly calculating in their head)* Hmmm. Seventy square feet. *(then to THE GUEST)* I can do it. Probably two months.

**KRIS:** Wait, how? With what?

**CHRIS:** There's a broker out by the port selling washed and ground HDPE flake by the sack. Post-industrial, clean, single polymer, consistent pigments. Forty cents a pound.

**KRIS:** So we'd be buying recycled material.

*CHRIS moves to the shelf, starts pulling mold trays, checking the shredder — already halfway to doing it.*

**CHRIS:** Yes, but we'd be *delivering*. Somebody wants a floor made out of recovered plastic instead of a floor made out of new plastic. At least it's not vinyl. This is waste plastic becoming a real, valuable, long life product. And we're going to lose it because the caps didn't arrive with the right story attached?

**KRIS:** *(not moving)* The story *is* the product.

**CHRIS:** The product is the product. People don't want a demonstration in their kitchen. They want a floor.

*Silence.*

**THE GUEST:** *(carefully)* Would it be the same?

**CHRIS:** Chemically? Identical.

**THE GUEST:** That's not what I meant.

*Neither of them answers. Silence.*

**KRIS:** Help us make a tile. Then ask again.

*Lights shift.*

## Scene III — The Making

*KRIS drags a crate to the shredder. The ketchup caps. All the same red.*

**CHRIS:** That's the ketchup cap collection.

**KRIS:** I know.

*KRIS switches on the shredder. It's loud. Conversation stops. Caps go in whole and come out as flake. It takes as long as it takes. If you are doing this for real, it should almost be too long.*

*KRIS shuts it off. Holds up a handful. Red, uniform, catching the light.*

**KRIS:** *(to THE GUEST)* That's forty years of one family's condiments.

*Pause.*

**THE GUEST:** Why plastic at all? Why not just stop?

**CHRIS:** Do we stop using plastic? New plastic, yes. Stop tomorrow.

**KRIS:** We're against wasting a permanent material on things that only get a few minutes of use. We have already made more plastic than we will ever need ever again. It's already here — in your bin, in the ground, in the sea, in that crate, blowing down the street outside. Let's use it.

**THE GUEST:** So there's not ever really a shortage of plastic?

**KRIS:** There's more than enough. And treated properly most resins can last forever. We built a material that lasts forever and then we used it once.

*Silence.*

*They tip the flake into a hopper.*

**KRIS:** It's to stop making new plastic out of oil, and take seriously the permanent material we already made.

**THE GUEST:** Meaning what, practically?

**KRIS:** Two things. Put it into objects solid enough and good enough that it stays put — a counter, a wall, a bench, a bedframe — so it isn't shedding microplastics out into the world. And when that object's finished, grind it up, take it back and make the next object. No need to ever pull up more oil.

*Pause. The extruder is up to temperature.*

**THE BOTTLE:** I am not a bottle. I am material that is temporarily a bottle.

**THE BOTTLE:** One million of me are bought every minute. Twenty thousand every second. While you have been sitting there.

**THE BOTTLE:** One of two things happens to me next.

**THE BOTTLE:** One, somebody keeps me separate, brings me here, and I am shredded, melted, and pressed. I become a bowl, a lamp, a tile. In forty years I become something else. The PET is unchanged.

**THE BOTTLE:** Two — and this is the average one — I go in a bin, or I end up floating in the water. The sun makes me brittle. I break into pieces. The pieces break into pieces.

**THE BOTTLE:** The bottle is finished either way. What happens after that is the only part anyone can still decide.

*CHRIS hands THE GUEST a scoop.*

**CHRIS:** Go on.

**THE GUEST:** I'll do it wrong.

**CHRIS:** Just scoop it slowly.

*THE GUEST feeds the extruder. Awkwardly at first. CHRIS adjusts nothing, lets them.*

*The machine changes pitch. A slow rope of red plastic begins to emerge.*

**THE GUEST:** Oh—

**CHRIS:** Yeah.

**THE GUEST:** It's warm.

**CHRIS:** Four hundred degrees warm. Don't.

*THE GUEST doesn't touch it. Watches.*

**THE GUEST:** I thought recycled plastic was — grey. Cheap-looking.

**KRIS:** It is, if you mix everything. All the colors together go brownish grey. That's what happens when you process by the ton — you just do it all at once.

**CHRIS:** Which is fine. For a fence post.

**KRIS:** We sort by color. By hand. Which means if you want red, we wait for red, and we have to painstakingly separate…

**THE GUEST:** How long does red take?

**KRIS:** That crate took somebody forty years.

**THE GUEST:** And if I want a specific color for the floor?

**CHRIS:** Then we're waiting on strangers to drink the right thing.

**KRIS:** Yes.

**CHRIS:** *(to THE GUEST)* Or I buy a sack of flake and you get your floor next month.

*Pause.*

**THE GUEST:** *(to KRIS)* And it's not stronger? Recycled?

**KRIS:** *(wiping down the press, not looking up)* It isn't harder. It isn't stronger. It's about the same, but if you get the polymers mixed it's worse. What it is, is honest. You can see what it's made of. You know where it came from because you were standing here. There is pretty much nothing else in your house you can say that about.

*Pause.*

**CHRIS:** *(tightly)* That's a harder sell.

**KRIS:** It's a true one.

*THE MICROPLASTIC speaks.*

**THE MICROPLASTIC:** Every gram held in a tile is a gram not in water, not in air, not in a body.

*KRIS transfers the extrusion into the sheet press. Closes it. Sets a weight.*

**KRIS:** Now we wait.

**THE GUEST:** How long?

**KRIS:** For this? a while… they stay hot for a bit.

*Nobody fills the silence. The press ticks as it cools. THE GUEST looks around the workshop — at the bins, the posters, the four solid jugs in the corner.*

**THE GUEST:** How long for my floor?

*KRIS doesn't answer yet. Opens the press. Lifts out a single tile — red, marbled, unmistakably made of what went in.*

*Hands it to THE GUEST. It's warm.*

*Long silence.*

**THE GUEST:** *(turning it over)* I'd keep this. I wouldn't throw this away. *(after a moment)* So, ask me again.

**KRIS:** Ask you what?

**THE GUEST:** You said ask me again after we made one. I'm asking.

*Lights shift.*

## Scene IV — The Offer

**KRIS:** Alright. Here's what I can offer you.

**KRIS:** You bring us your plastic. Yours. Out of your house. Rinsed, sorted by number. We hold it. When there's enough, we make your tiles out of that.

*Pause.*

**THE GUEST:** My own waste.

**KRIS:** Right, your own material.

**THE GUEST:** How long do you think we need to collect?

**KRIS:** I don't know.

**THE GUEST:** You must have some idea.

**KRIS:** I have no idea, because I don't know what your house uses and throws away in a week. Do you know what you generate plastic waste wise?

**THE GUEST:** I could guess.

**KRIS:** Then guess.

**THE GUEST:** *(Pause.)* A bag a week?

**KRIS:** The average American throws out about three hundred pounds of plastic a year. Per person. People who guess about their house are always wrong beforehand. Always low.

**THE GUEST:** So I'd count it.

**KRIS:** For a week. Everything. Then we'd know.

**CHRIS:** And then it's still years.

**KRIS:** Who knows.

**CHRIS:** *(to THE GUEST)* Not two months. Years. My colleague is offering you homework and a waiting.

**THE GUEST:** Years of saving milk bottles and whatever else for a kitchen floor.

**CHRIS:** For a very good kitchen floor.

*THE GUEST is looking at the tile. Stops.*

**THE GUEST:** Hang on.

**KRIS:** Mm.

*KRIS waits.*

**THE GUEST:** People walk on floors. In shoes. With grit on them. Chairs get dragged. That's — that's abrasion, isn't it. I'd be laying down seventy square feet of it and then standing and walking and scraping on it every day…

*Silence.*

**KRIS:** Yes. That's how microplastics get made too — not just from what gets thrown away.

*THE MICROPLASTIC speaks.*

**THE MICROPLASTIC:** I am also this. Whatever wears off a floor, day after day, is me too.

**THE GUEST:** Wait, the tiles in the café aren't a floor. They're behind the coffee machine. On the wall.

**KRIS:** Nobody stands on a wall.

**THE GUEST:** So why did you let me talk about a floor for an hour?

**KRIS:** You needed to see it yourself.

*Silence. CHRIS is the one who looks uncomfortable.*

**THE GUEST:** Backsplash? Behind the sink. That's — what, ten square feet?

**KRIS:** About that.

**THE GUEST:** And how much time is ten square feet?

**KRIS:** Just count your plastic for a week. Then I'll tell you.

**THE GUEST:** In my current bin, what happens to it now? What I put out for pickup?

**KRIS:** About five percent of it gets recycled. The rest is landfilled or burned.

**THE GUEST:** Five percent.

**KRIS:** Five.

**THE GUEST:** Ugh.

*Long silence. THE GUEST's eyes drift to a poster on the wall — doesn't read it, not yet.*

**CHRIS:** Can we get back to the other thing?

*CHRIS is already pulling a sack of flake toward the shredder as he says it.*

**CHRIS:** I take the café job. Forty more tiles. Broker flake, done soon, the café pays us. That money keeps the lights on and the machines running. And with the lights on — *(to THE GUEST)* — we do yours properly. Your material. However long it takes. We can afford to be slow about yours because of the proper café job.

*KRIS says nothing.*

**KRIS:** *(eventually)* The café tiles get labeled. What's in them, where it came from.

**CHRIS:** Fine.

**KRIS:** Every one.

**CHRIS:** I said fine.

*They both look at THE GUEST.*

*Pause.*

**THE GUEST:** Okay? We're doing it?

**KRIS:** That's for you to decide, not us.

*THE GUEST looks at the tile. At the bins. At the four solid jugs in the corner.*

**THE GUEST:** How do I count it?

*Pause. KRIS looks at CHRIS.*

**CHRIS:** *(a small concession)* There's a form. I'll print you a form. It helps with the codes and colors and such…

**THE GUEST:** And I bring it back in a week.

**KRIS:** Yes, fill out Chris's form and bring us your plastic in a week.

**THE GUEST:** *(holding up the tile)* Can I keep this tile?

**KRIS:** Sure, that one's yours. You made it.

*Lights shift.*

---

## Scene V — The Door Again

*THE GUEST at the door, holding the tile.*

**THE GUEST:** Okay, so one last time, what do I bring?

**KRIS:** All of it. Everything plastic that would otherwise exit your house for a week.

**THE GUEST:** Even the stuff you can't use?

**KRIS:** …We try.

*THE GUEST looks back at the room once — the bins, the shelf of tiles, the posters, the four solid jugs in the corner…*

*Pause.*

**THE GUEST:** *(reading the wall)* Look at your plastic.

*The guest leaves. The door closes.*

*KRIS goes back to the sorting table. CHRIS checks a delivery slip for the café order, satisfied, then goes back to the shredder. Neither says anything for a moment. Silence.*

**CHRIS:** They won't come back.

**KRIS:** Some do.

*The shredder runs. Then stops.*

*The door opens. A NEW GUEST steps in.*

**NEW GUEST:** Sorry — are you open? I'm looking for tiles.

*CHRIS turns around immediately. KRIS does not.*

**CHRIS:** We're open.

*KRIS, still sorting, allows the smallest smile.*

*From somewhere in the room:*

**THE MICROPLASTIC:** I am a plastic fragment under five millimeters.

**THE MICROPLASTIC:** I am still here.

*Long silence.*

*THE NEW GUEST comes further in. Picks up a tile from the shelf. Turns it over. There's a number on the bottom.*

---

*END. BEGINNING.*

# Notes on Sources

Every figure spoken in this play is checked. Notes are keyed to the line as spoken. Where a source is dated, the year is given — some widely-cited figures are several years old and the underlying numbers have since grown.

---

### Scene I — The Order

**1. "Days in a truck. Weeks on a shelf. Minutes in a hand. Centuries after that."**
Distribution and shelf time: Coda Plastics, *The Life Cycle of a Plastic Bottle*. Use time — "less than five minutes to swig back the contents": World Economic Forum, summarizing NOAA. Breakdown time for a PET bottle ≈ 450 years: NOAA Marine Debris Program and Woods Hole Sea Grant estimates.

**2. "I am PET."**
Clear beverage bottles are polyethylene terephthalate, Resin Identification Code #1. ASTM International D7611.

**3. "Fewer than half of us are collected. Of the ones collected, seven percent come back as bottles."**
Euromonitor International global packaging data, reported in *The Guardian*, 28 June 2017. **These figures are from 2017.** Global bottle sales have risen since — 480 billion in 2016, projected 583 billion by 2021 — so the collection and closed-loop percentages are, if anything, conservative.

**4. "That's a two. HDPE."**
High-density polyethylene, Resin Identification Code #2. ASTM D7611. Note that an RIC identifies polymer type only; it is **not** a claim that an item is recyclable or will be recycled. This distinction is the subject of ongoing regulatory attention.

**5. "Somebody dropped off a crate of these… forty years of one family's ketchup."**
Plastic squeeze bottles for ketchup were introduced in the early 1980s, making a forty-year household accumulation plausible. Note that ketchup closures — particularly flip-tops with living hinges — are typically **polypropylene (PP, #5)**, not HDPE. PP and HDPE together account for roughly 90% of bottle caps worldwide.

**6. Milk jug arithmetic — "That one's fifty-five… about the same as a full one."**
A 1 US gallon jug holds 3.785 L. HDPE density ≈ 0.95 g/cm³, so a solid form of the same external volume weighs ≈ 3.6 kg (7.9 lb). An empty jug weighs ≈ 65 g, giving ≈ **55 jugs** per solid form and ≈ **220** for four. A full gallon of milk weighs ≈ 3.9 kg (8.6 lb) — hence "about the same as a full one."

**7. Tile and floor arithmetic — "About twenty-five thousand caps."**
An HDPE cap weighs ≈ 2.3 g; 90 caps ≈ 207 g ≈ one 6″ × 6″ tile at ~10 mm thickness. At 4 tiles per square foot, that is ~360 caps per square foot. Seventy square feet ≈ **25,200 caps**. At 40 caps collected per week, ≈ **12 years**. Ten square feet (the backsplash) ≈ 3,600 caps ≈ **90 weeks**.

---

### Scene II — The Misclassification

**8. "Vinyl plank… PVC. Resin code three."**
Luxury vinyl plank is constructed primarily from polyvinyl chloride. Resin Identification Code #3.

**9. "One of the hardest plastics there is to recover — almost none of it ever gets recycled."**
LVP's multi-layer construction — wear layer, printed film, PVC core, backing, adhesives — makes separation impractical. Most material recovery facilities are not equipped to process post-consumer vinyl flooring.

**10. "Four dollars a square foot. Three hundred bucks."**
2026 US material costs: LVP $2–5/sq ft; ceramic tile $1–5; solid red oak $3–8. Installed costs roughly double. Seventy square feet at $4 = $280.

**11. "This doesn't usually survive that. It's too small."**
Small-format items, particularly loose closures, fall through disc screens at material recovery facilities and report to residue. This is why "leave the cap on the bottle" has become standard guidance — newer facilities can process capped bottles, separating PET (which sinks) from HDPE and PP (which float) by density in a wash bath.

**12. "The glass shatters on the way in… broken glass in everything."**
Glass breakage is intrinsic to single-stream collection and processing. Broken glass contaminates paper and plastic bales and drives abrasive wear on sorting equipment. Glass fines constitute the majority of single-stream residue.

**13. "One bin is cheaper to collect than three."**
Single-stream collection was adopted primarily for collection economics — fewer vehicles, reduced labor, higher household participation. Recovery quality was the trade-off.

**14. "I am a plastic fragment under five millimeters."**
Standard definition of a microplastic.

**15. "Polyester is PET. Resin code one. The same polymer as a water bottle."**
Correct. Polyester fiber and PET bottle resin are the same polymer.

**16. "One load of polyester releases half a million fibers."**
Napper, I. E. & Thompson, R. C. (2016). "Release of synthetic microplastic plastic fibres from domestic washing machines: Effects of fabric type and washing conditions." *Marine Pollution Bulletin* 112(1–2), 39–45. doi:10.1016/j.marpolbul.2016.09.025
A 6 kg load released approximately **496,000 fibers** for polyester, 729,000 for acrylic, and 138,000 for a polyester-cotton blend. Measured as release into wastewater.

**17. "Polyester is fifty-nine percent of all fiber made on earth."**
Textile Exchange, *Materials Market Report 2025* (2024 data). Polyester: 59% of global fiber production, 78 million tonnes. All synthetics: 69%. Cotton: 19%. Notably, textile-to-textile recycling remains below 1% of the market, and 98% of "recycled polyester" clothing is made from plastic bottles rather than from old clothes.

**18. "I have been found in human blood. In lungs. In breast milk."**
Blood — Leslie, H. A. et al. (2022), *Environment International* 163:107199. doi:10.1016/j.envint.2022.107199
Lungs — Jenner, L. C. et al. (2022), *Science of the Total Environment* 831:154907. doi:10.1016/j.scitotenv.2022.154907
Breast milk — Ragusa, A. et al. (2022), *Polymers* 14(13):2700. doi:10.3390/polym14132700

**19. "Particles under ten micrometers cross the placental barrier."**
Ragusa, A. et al. (2021), "Plasticenta: First evidence of microplastics in human placenta," *Environment International* 146:106274. doi:10.1016/j.envint.2020.106274
On barrier crossing specifically: *Frontiers in Endocrinology* (2024), doi:10.3389/fendo.2024.1481014. Systematic review of in-utero exposure: PMC11186737.

**20. "Forty cents a pound" (post-industrial HDPE flake).**
Within the normal range for washed post-industrial HDPE flake, generally $0.30–0.60/lb. Not tied to a specific published index.

### Scene III — The Making

**21. "One million of me are bought every minute. Twenty thousand every second."**
Euromonitor International, reported in *The Guardian*, 2017 — see note 3. **Figures are from 2017** and have since increased.

**22. "We have already made more plastic than we will ever need."**
Global production has more than doubled since 2000 and continues to rise. Approximately 9% of all plastic ever produced has been recycled — OECD, *Global Plastics Outlook* (2022).

**23. "Four hundred degrees warm."**
HDPE melts at 120–130°C and is extruded at 180–240°C (356–464°F). Four hundred degrees Fahrenheit ≈ 204°C, within the normal extrusion window. Thermal degradation begins above 300°C.

**24. "It isn't harder. It isn't stronger. It's about the same, but if you get the polymers mixed it's worse."**
Correct, and worth preserving against the common overclaim. Reprocessing shortens polymer chains, so recyclate is equal to or slightly below virgin material in mechanical performance. Mixed polymers perform worse than either constituent.

### Scene IV — The Offer

**25. "The average American throws out about three hundred pounds of plastic a year."**
Greenpeace USA (2022): 51 million tons of plastic waste discarded in the United States in 2021. Divided across a population of approximately 332 million, this is ≈ **307 lb per person per year**.

**26. "About five percent of it gets recycled. The rest is landfilled or burned."**
Beyond Plastics and The Last Beach Cleanup, *The Real Truth About the U.S. Plastics Recycling Rate* (2022): 5–6%. The most recent official EPA figure is 8.7%, for 2018, predating the full effect of China's National Sword import restrictions. Of the remainder, approximately 86% is landfilled and 9% incinerated — which is why the American version of this line names landfill before burning.

**27. "People walk on floors. In shoes. With grit on them."**
Abrasion of plastic surfaces in use is a recognized source of microplastic generation, distinct from the fragmentation of discarded plastic in the environment. This is why the play's guest, on realizing it, redirects from a floor to a backsplash.

### General

**Resin Identification Codes** were introduced by the Society of the Plastics Industry in 1988 and are now administered by ASTM International as standard D7611. The standard states explicitly that the presence of an RIC does not imply that an item is recycled, or that systems exist to recycle it. Surveys consistently find that a large majority of consumers believe otherwise.

**On the circular economy framework** — "waste is a design flaw," the technical and biological cycles, and circulating materials at their highest value: Ellen MacArthur Foundation, *Circular Economy Introduction*. ellenmacarthurfoundation.org

**On the workshop model** — machines, processes, and open-source documentation: Precious Plastic. preciousplastic.com
