# A Carbon Sequestering Book — essay notes

Raw notes from a working session, 2026-09-27. These are notes, not prose: the ideas to draw on, not the draft itself. Pointer: `lectureScripts/projectWriteups/carbon-sequestering-book.md`.

The published case study (`a.wjerk.shop/case-study-carbon-sequestering-book.html`) already has the argument's pieces. Quarry it; don't rewrite it.

## A possible spine

- **The book as an instrument.** How dark the print is shows how much carbon it has fixed. Reading becomes measuring. A stronger idea than “green book.”
- **Be honest about scale.** Ca(OH)₂ + CO₂ → CaCO₃ takes up about 0.6 g of CO₂ per gram of hydroxide (44/74). A book's ink is a few grams, and making a book emits on the order of 1–2 kg CO₂e (check a source). So the book is a demonstration you can see, not a carbon sink. Say so outright, the same move as “it makes it less bad.”
- **Cradle to Cradle → The Upcycle.** The supply chain has to exist. Pair it with “where it led”: a book that doesn't exist helped start an organization that does (Climate Designers).

## Materials to research (none verified yet)

- **Biochar as pigment.** Carbon black from pyrolysed biomass instead of fossil fuel. The pigment *is* the stored carbon, stable for centuries. Possibly the most real option.
- **AIR-INK (Graviky Labs).** Ink made from captured diesel soot. A precedent for “the pigment used to be pollution.”
- **Olivine and enhanced weathering.** Takes up CO₂ as it weathers. A green mineral pigment, so the ink could weather along with the book.
- **Living cyanobacteria.** Living Ink uses dead algae. A living photosynthetic binder is the more speculative step.
- **Amine sorbents and MOFs.** Direct-air-capture chemistry. Reversible by design (see “One direction”), so they're the wrong family for the ink. Useful only as contrast.
- **Alkaline-buffered archival paper.** Already in the case study and under-used: libraries hold millions of books quietly doing this chemistry.

## Precedents (verified)

- *El libro que no puede esperar* / *The Book That Can't Wait*, Eterna Cadencia with draftFCB, Buenos Aires, 2012. An anthology of new Latin American writers, sold sealed airtight. Light and air erase the ink within two to four months of opening. The reverse of the carbon book, with the same trigger. The sealed packaging solves the carbon book's problem too: keep the ink clear until the reader opens it. https://www.designboom.com/design/the-book-that-cant-wait-is-printed-in-disappearing-ink/ (Designboom, 27 June 2012)
- *Visionaire 56 Solar*, sponsored by Calvin Klein Collection, printed through Imago. Photochromic plastisol ink: invisible indoors, full color in direct sun, fades again indoors, so it's **reversible**. Alex Katz, Peter Lindbergh, Ryan McGinley, David Sims, Roe Ethridge. https://imagogroup.com/case-studies/visionaire-56-photochromic-ink/
- *The Outside Book*, Fruitella (a Brazilian snack brand), 2024. A kids' book in sun-activated ink that you can only read outdoors. https://rohitbhargava.com/the-new-kids-book-that-can-only-be-read-while-outside-thanks-to-sun-activated-ink/ (Trend Hunter's page blocks automated fetching.)
- “4442 book”, Trend Hunter, photosensitive ink. Not checked, since the page blocks fetching. https://www.trendhunter.com/trends/4442-book
- *The Sun Will Set Into My Palms*, Phoebe Nightingale (around 2018, going by the image metadata). An artist's book whose detachable cover is a cyanotype. It changes color while you read in sunlight and leaves a print of the reader's hands. **Permanent and cumulative**, unlike Visionaire: each copy keeps a record of how it was read. https://www.phoebenightingale.com/cyanotype-book
  - Carbon twist: breath is ~40,000 ppm CO₂. A carbon-reactive page could record where the reader breathed on it, so each copy would carry the reader's own carbon. (It's short-cycle carbon, so it's a record rather than drawdown.)
- Takeaway: ink that reacts to light is a solved, printable thing, with reversible effects and in trade editions. Ink that reacts to carbon is the step nobody has taken.

## One direction (Kristian, 2026-09-27)

What he liked about the Nightingale cover: it only goes one way. Cyanotype doesn't come back. That's the property a sequestering ink needs too, and it sorts every precedent and every chemistry into two groups:

- **One-way** (records, keeps): cyanotype · lime → limestone (mineralization) · *The Book That Can't Wait* (erasure is one-way too). This is the sequestering book. The darkness is the carbon, and it stays.
  - Most natural CO₂ uptake belongs here, and it's slow and cumulative: lime plaster curing, olivine weathering (Project Vesta), CO₂ into stone, concrete carbonation (Carbicrete, CarbonCure), biochar. All of those are already in the channel. Their timescale (months to years) is a book's timescale. The ink wouldn't have to be fast, only one-way.
- **Reversible** (reports, then resets): Visionaire's photochromic ink · pH-dye CO₂ indicators. This is the threshold book. It works as a gauge and stores nothing.
  - Direct-air-capture sorbents (amines, MOFs) are in this group *on purpose*: they grab CO₂, then get heated to release it for storage and are reused. That makes them the wrong family for the ink.

The case study already draws this line: “The pH route responds to CO₂ concentration but doesn't permanently lock carbon away; the mineralization route would.” The direction of the chemistry is the claim the book makes.

## Getting to dark (2026-09-27)

Carbonates are white. Black carbon is *reduced* carbon, and getting CO₂ back to it takes energy. Routes that keep the carbon *and* go dark:

- **Photosynthesis.** Biological soil crusts darken as they mature because cyanobacteria (heterocystous Nostocales) build up scytonemin, a yellow-brown sunscreen pigment. Mature crusts absorb more light and run up to 10 °C warmer. https://www.nature.com/articles/ncomms10373 · https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7997468/ · They also recover pigment quickly after drying out and being rewetted: https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0112372
- **Electrochemistry.** Esrafilzadeh et al., “Room temperature CO₂ reduction to solid carbon species on liquid metals featuring atomically thin ceria interfaces,” *Nature Communications*, 26 Feb 2019 (RMIT). Gallium-cerium liquid metal plus a small current turns CO₂ into solid carbon flakes. https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-019-08824-8
- **Biochar pigment.** Dark from day one. The drawdown happened in the field before printing.

## Side idea: the threshold book (Kristian, 2026-09-27)

The book is illegible while atmospheric CO₂ is too high. It only becomes readable once *everyone* has drawn carbon down. The book doesn't sequester anything. It waits.

- The threshold could be 350 ppm (the “safe” line, and 350.org's name). It's about 420+ now. You'd print a book today that nobody can read until the world gets back there.
- Real starting point: colorimetric CO₂ indicators. These are pH dyes that change color with CO₂. They're used in hospital breath detectors (end-tidal CO₂) and in food-packaging freshness indicators, and they reverse like Visionaire's ink. But they respond to percent-level CO₂. Telling 420 ppm from 350 ppm is the unsolved part. [verify]
- Complications that help the piece: indoor air runs 600–1,000+ ppm, so like *The Outside Book* it can only be read outdoors. Exhaled breath is ~40,000 ppm, so leaning in to read it would fog the page. You'd have to hold your breath.
- The Keeling Curve (already in the channel) is the book's clock.
- This fits the honest-scale problem: the book doesn't have to be a carbon sink if it's a gauge.
