# A Book That Draws Down Carbon

Rough draft, 2026-09-27. Notes, sources and side ideas: `CarbonSequesteringBook.md`. Pointer: `lectureScripts/projectWriteups/carbon-sequestering-book.md`. [Brackets] are open questions.

---

Fresh Press makes paper out of agricultural waste and prairie grass. Eric Benson helped start it at the University of Illinois. The paper is carbon-neutral, sometimes carbon-negative. Then someone prints on it with ink made from petroleum.

The rest of the book is close to solved. Paper from grass. Glue from wheat starch or hide, the way bookbinders made it for centuries. Board from recycled fiber. Each of those is a purchasing decision someone could make today. The ink is the problem.

Years ago I saw a book printed in ink that started out clear. [Which one? Visionaire, The Outside Book, the “4442” book, or something else?] To read it you had to take it outside. The type only came up after a while in real sun. Indoor light wasn't enough.

Books like that aren't a complete anomoly. *Visionaire 56 Solar* used photochromic ink that bloomed into color in direct sun and faded again indoors. In 2024 a Brazilian snack brand, Fruitella, put out *The Outside Book*, a kids' book you could only read outdoors. Phoebe Nightingale's *The Sun Will Set Into My Palms* has a cyanotype cover. It changes color while you read it in sunlight and leaves a print of your hands (That one doesn't revert back! cyantotypes are only one direection!).

What I like about all of them is what they make the reader do. The design force the reader to go do something, you have to go outside. A printed object sets up a relationship between a person and the weather. Nightingale's goes furthest, because it keeps a real record. The outdoor trip happened and the cover "remembers" it.

In 2012 a small Buenos Aires publisher, Eterna Cadencia, sold an anthology of new writers sealed in airtight plastic. Once you opened it, light and air started erasing the ink. In two to four months the pages were blank. It was called *The Book That Can't Wait*. The argument was simple: if nobody reads a writer's first book, there won't be a second. The carbon book runs the same clock backwards. It ships sealed too. Open it and it starts to fill in.

So: an ink that takes carbon dioxide out of the air and keeps it, and darkens as it does. The book gets more readable as it stores more carbon. Drawdown is the climate word for the point where carbon in the atmosphere starts to fall.

Most of the ways the planet takes up carbon already work like the cyanotype. They go one way, slowly. Lime plaster hardens by pulling CO₂ out of the air and turning back into limestone. Roman walls are still doing it. Crushed olivine weathers and locks CO₂ into rock. Concrete carbonates for decades.

The catch is color. Carbon locked into stone is often white. Chalk, limestone, shells, plaster. Carbon is typically black when it is on its own: charcoal, graphite, soot. Pulling it back out takes energy.

Plants do it with sunlight. So the sun comes back into it. In deserts, cyanobacteria grow into crusts on bare soil. As a crust matures it builds up a sunscreen pigment called scytonemin, and the ground turns dark. A living ink could do the same. It prints nearly clear. You take the book outside, and the type darkens as it grows, made out of the carbon it took in. [How alive can a page be? It would need water, or it needs to be the kind of crust that dries out and wakes back up when it's wet.]

There's a version without anything alive. In 2019 researchers at RMIT in Melbourne ran CO₂ through liquid gallium with a trace of cerium, passed a small current through it, and got flakes of solid black carbon at room temperature. Coal, more or less, from air. A page can't hold a beaker of liquid metal. [A page with a solar cell? Or is that one step too far for this piece?]

The numbers are small. The type in a book is a few grams of ink. Making the book put a kilo or two into the air. [Verify the per-book figure.] The book never pays itself back. It's a gauge. How dark the page is shows how much carbon it has taken in.

McDonough and Braungart printed *Cradle to Cradle* on plastic pages meant to be recycled forever. No one was set up to collect them. Their next book went back to paper. A new ink needs a publisher who controls every material in the book: an artist's book, an institution, a small edition. *The Book That Can't Wait* and *Visionaire* were both small, controlled editions. That's where this starts too.

I still haven't found the ink.

Clear ink. Go outside. Wait.
