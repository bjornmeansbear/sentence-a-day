# People Processing Plastic
## A play in five scenes

---

*People come in.*
*Conversations happen.*
*Someone leaves changed.*
*The door opens again.*

---

## A Note on Production

Preferably, one would somehow perform this in a working plastic workshop. The shredder, the extruder, the press are best when not props — when they run, they're loud, and the room smells of warm plastic. The tile made during the performance is real… 

The Bottle and the Microplastic are documentary voices. They state facts about their own existence. They're triggered by words in the human conversation — someone says *microplastics*, the Microplastic speaks. They never introduce themselves. 

All roles are written for any performer. No character has a gender.

---

We are not anti-plastic. We're against wasting a permanent material on a few minutes of use.

---

## Cast

**KRIS** — Founded the workshop. Believes the objects are an argument, and that where the material came from is most of the argument. Would rather send someone away with an idea than a sale.

**CHRIS** — Believes the objects are the argument, and an argument nobody buys isn't one. Wants to deliver, however they can.

**THE GUEST** — Came to buy tiles for a kitchen floor. Seventy square feet.

**THE NEW GUEST** — Arrives at the end.

**THE BOTTLE** & **THE MICROPLASTIC** — Expository Voices.

---

## Scene I — The Order

*A Precious Plastic workshop. Shredder, extruder warming up, sorting table. Bins labeled HDPE, PET, PP. A shelf of finished tiles — multicolored, marbled. Four dense grey-blue forms in the corner, the shape of milk jugs. On the walls: hand-made posters, resin codes, a circle diagram. Near the door: PPP — People Processing Plastic.*

*KRIS at the sorting table. CHRIS at the shredder, feeding in bottle caps — red, blue, white.*

**THE BOTTLE:** I am made from material that can last forever.

**THE BOTTLE:** But, do you know how long the average bottle lasts?

**THE BOTTLE:** Days in a truck. Weeks on a shelf.

**THE BOTTLE:** Minutes in a hand.

**THE BOTTLE:** Centuries after that.

**THE BOTTLE:** More time was spent moving me to you than you spent using me.

**THE BOTTLE:** I am PET. Fewer than half of us are collected. Of the ones collected, seven percent come back as bottles.

**THE BOTTLE:** The rest become something lesser. Or nothing.

*The shredder runs. CHRIS shuts it off. Silence, suddenly.*

**CHRIS:** The café wants forty more.

**KRIS:** We already made them their twenty.

**CHRIS:** They liked the twenty. They want more.

**KRIS:** *(still sorting)* We're not a tile company.

**CHRIS:** We're not an anything, except maybe a performance. That's the problem.

**KRIS:** Yeah, you're right, we're a demonstration.

**CHRIS:** A demonstration nobody's paying for.

*KRIS holds up a piece of plastic. Reads the number on the bottom. Sets it into a bin. CHRIS picks up a recently dropped off bag of bottles*

**KRIS:** That bag's mixed. Sort it before it goes in.

**CHRIS:** It's mostly HDPE.

**KRIS:** The numbers on the bottles aren't based on "mostly."

*CHRIS pulls the bag. Sorts it. This exchange has clearly happened before.*

**CHRIS:** *(holding up a red cap)* Somebody dropped off a crate of these. All the same red. Forty years of one family's ketchup bottles.

**KRIS:** *(a beat, genuinely)* Huh.

**CHRIS:** Do you reckon its enough for a tabletop. That'd be a tabletop with a good story.

**KRIS:** That's a hundred and fifty conversations.

*The door opens. THE GUEST steps in.*

**THE GUEST:** Sorry — are you open? I'm looking for tiles.

*CHRIS turns around immediately, suddenly smiling. KRIS does not.*

**CHRIS:** Yes! We're open.

**THE GUEST:** I saw the tiles at a café around the corner. Behind the espresso machine — the whole wall of them. I asked where they came from and somebody told me to look for the smiling recycling symbol?

**CHRIS:** That's us.

**THE GUEST:** I want to do my kitchen floor.

*A pause. KRIS stops sorting.*

**KRIS:** How much floor?

**THE GUEST:** About seventy square feet. It's a galley. It's tiny.

*KRIS and CHRIS look at each other. Something passes between them that THE GUEST can't read.*

**CHRIS:** Seventy square feet.

**THE GUEST:** Is that a lot?

**CHRIS:** *(picking up a handful of flakes)* Depends on what you're measuring I guess? It definitely will need a lot of flakes!

*THE GUEST comes further in. Looks at the shelf. Picks up a tile. Turns it over. There's a number molded into the bottom.*

**THE GUEST:** What's the number?

**CHRIS:** That's it's Resin code. Every piece of plastic has one. Tells you what it's made of. That's a two. HDPE. Anything we remake we renumber correctly.

**THE GUEST:** And the bins?

**CHRIS:** One number per bin. Keep them separate, you keep the value. Mix them—

**KRIS:** Landfill.

**THE GUEST:** So this was — what, bottles?

**CHRIS:** That one was mostly caps. Different colors because different brands. Same material. That's how we get the heathering and marbling.

**THE GUEST:** Tiles out of trash. Cool.

*A small pause. They've heard this word.*

**CHRIS:** Waste. Trash is a decision. Waste is material between purposes.

**THE GUEST:** How many caps in one tile?

**CHRIS:** Depends on thickness. We can make them to preference. That one, maybe ninety.

**THE GUEST:** And seventy square feet?

*CHRIS doesn't answer immediately. KRIS answers instead.*

**KRIS:** About twenty-five thousand caps.

*THE GUEST looks at the bins. Then at the tile in their hand.*

**THE GUEST:** How many do you have?

**KRIS:** Today? Maybe two thousand. 

**THE GUEST:** So how long?

**KRIS:** Depends what people bring us. Or how particular for a color or pattern you are.

**THE GUEST:** That's not an answer.

**KRIS:** Not really… but it's the way this works.

*THE GUEST moves further in, uncertain now. Stops at the corner — four solid forms, whitish grey-blue, marbled. Crouches. Lifts one. Surprised at heft.*

**THE GUEST:** What are these?

**CHRIS:** Milk jugs.

**THE GUEST:** It's solid.

**CHRIS:** Yeah. Milk jugs MADE of solid milk jugs.

**THE GUEST:** How many?

**CHRIS:** That one's fifty-five. Those four together, two hundred and twenty. People kept bringing them in. I needed something to do with some excess. That seemed clever.

**THE GUEST:** How much does it weigh?

**CHRIS:** About the same as a full one.

*THE GUEST stands. Looks at it differently.*

**THE GUEST:** So can you make my floor tiles or not?

*KRIS and CHRIS look at each other again.*

**CHRIS:** Yes.

**KRIS:** No.

*Lights shift.*

---

## Scene II — The Misclassification

*Nobody moves. THE GUEST looks between them.*

**THE GUEST:** Is that a joke?

**CHRIS:** No.

**KRIS:** We disagree about what this place is.

**CHRIS:** *(to KRIS)* Twenty-five thousand caps at forty a week is—

**KRIS:** Twelve years.

**CHRIS:** If we put the word out it's not twelve years. The ketchup caps crate came in off one flyer.

**KRIS:** And then we're a floor company with a waiting list.

**CHRIS:** Yeah! We'd be a company. That's not the insult you think it is.

*THE GUEST, awkward, still holding the tile.*

**THE GUEST:** I could just — I mean, I could drive out to Floor & Decor and buy tile?

**KRIS:** You could. What would you get?

**THE GUEST:** I don't know. Whatever's on sale.

**KRIS:** That would be vinyl plank probably. Four dollars a square foot. Three hundred bucks, delivered Thursday.

*A beat.*

**KRIS:** You know what vinyl plank is?

**THE GUEST:** ...Vinyl?

**KRIS:** Yeah, vinyl, PVC. Resin code three. You'd be buying a plastic floor. Brand new plastic, crude oil a couple months ago. And it's one of the hardest plastics there is to recover — almost none of it ever gets recycled.

**THE GUEST:** So I'd be buying a plastic floor either way.

*KRIS goes back to sorting. Doesn't answer.*

**THE GUEST:** Can I ask something? I recycle. Properly. Everything gets rinsed. Why isn't that enough?

*KRIS stops sorting.*

**KRIS:** What bin do the caps go in?

**THE GUEST:** Same one. Everything goes in the same one.

**KRIS:** Single stream. One truck, one bin, sorted later by machine.

**THE GUEST:** Right.

**KRIS:** *(holding up a red cap)* This doesn't usually survive that. It's too small. At the facility it drops through the screens with the glass shards and the grit and goes out as residue.

*A beat.*

**THE GUEST:** The caps.

**CHRIS:** All of them. Every cap you've ever rinsed. I mean, unless you left it screwed on the bottle... which is its own separate set of problems since usually a cap is HDPE and the bottles they're on are PET... I digress.

**KRIS:** And the glass shatters on the way in — I mean, that's partially how the line separates it — but now there's broken glass in everything. In the paper bales. In the plastic. Abrasive contamination drives maintenance costs up sharply.

**THE GUEST:** So why do it that way?

**KRIS:** Because one bin is cheaper to collect than three. More people “participate,” the truck goes to one place, labour costs drop. It's an excellent collection system. 

**THE GUEST:** But not a recovery system.

**KRIS:** It was optimised for ease of collection. Not for the materials.

*THE GUEST sets down the tile. Picks up a bottle from the nearest bin, turns it over, checks the number. Neither of them comments, but both notice.*

**THE GUEST:** And plastics that don't get sorted? that don't make it to the next steps?

**CHRIS:** They get small.

**THE GUEST:** Small?

**KRIS:** Plastic doesn't rot. It breaks down. Into pieces too small to sort, too small to collect, too small to see. But, still plastic, still permanent.

*A beat.*

**THE MICROPLASTIC:** I am a plastic fragment under five millimetres.

**THE MICROPLASTIC:** I am being made right now. By the polyester shirt on your back. Polyester is PET. Resin code one. The same polymer as a water bottle.

**THE MICROPLASTIC:** One load of polyester releases half a million fibres. Polyester is fifty-nine percent of all fibre made on earth.

**THE MICROPLASTIC:** I am also every carrier bag that went brittle in the sun. Every length of polypropylene rope that frayed in the water. 

**THE MICROPLASTIC:** I am what happens to plastic when it's thrown away. I am what happens while plastic is being used.

**THE MICROPLASTIC:** I have been found in human blood. In lungs. In breast milk. Particles under ten micrometres cross the placental barrier. I was in your body before your body was born.

*THE GUEST is very still.*

**THE GUEST:** Did you say something?

**CHRIS:** *(not looking up)* Extruder was warming up.

*THE GUEST doesn't believe them. Lets it go.*

**THE GUEST:** So a floor made of this — that's plastic that isn't just going out lose into the world.

**KRIS:** *(carefully)* Yes.

**CHRIS:** *(to KRIS)* Say the rest of it.

**KRIS:** Seventy square feet of tile is twenty-five thousand caps that stay solid instead. That's not nothing.

**CHRIS:** It's the entire argument.

**KRIS:** It's half the argument.

**CHRIS:** *(to THE GUEST)* Seventy square feet. I can do it. Two months.

**KRIS:** With what?

**CHRIS:** There's a broker out by the port selling washed HDPE flake by the sack. Post-industrial, clean, single polymer, better than anything that walks through that door. Forty cents a pound.

**KRIS:** So we'd be buying material.

**CHRIS:** Yes, but we'd be *delivering*. Somebody wants a floor made out of recovered plastic instead of a floor made out of new plastic. That's the win. That's the entire thing we say we're for. And we're going to lose it because the caps didn't arrive with the right story attached?

**KRIS:** The story *is* the product.

**CHRIS:** The product is the product. People don't want a demonstration in their kitchen. They want a floor.

*Silence.*

**THE GUEST:** *(carefully)* Would it be the same?

**CHRIS:** Chemically? Identical.

**THE GUEST:** That's not what I asked.

*Neither of them answers.*

**KRIS:** You should see how it's made first. Then ask again.

*Lights shift.*

---

## Scene III — The Making

*KRIS drags a crate to the shredder. Bottle caps. All the same red.*

**CHRIS:** That's the ketchup cap collection.

**KRIS:** I know.

*KRIS switches on the shredder. It's loud. Conversation stops. Caps go in whole and come out as flake. It takes as long as it takes.*

*KRIS shuts it off. Holds up a handful. Red, uniform, catching the light.*

**KRIS:** *(to THE GUEST)* That's forty years of one family's condiments.

**THE GUEST:** Why plastic at all? Why not just stop?

**CHRIS:** New plastic, yes. Stop tomorrow.

**KRIS:** But this plastic? well, we've already made it. That's the part people skip. We have already made more plastic than we will ever need ever again. It's already here — in your bin, in the ground, in the sea, in that crate, blowing down the street outside.

**THE GUEST:** So there's enough.

**KRIS:** There's more than enough. And it lasts forever. We built a material that lasts forever and then we used it once.

*They tip the flake into a hopper.*

**KRIS:** So the job isn't to stop using plastic. It's to stop making new plastic out of oil, and take seriously the permanent material we already made.

**THE GUEST:** Meaning what, practically?

**KRIS:** Two things. Put it into objects solid enough and good enough that it stays put — a counter, a wall, a bench — so it isn't shedding into anything. And when that object's finished, take it back and make the next one. Forever. Without ever pulling up more oil.

*A beat. The extruder is up to temperature.*

**THE BOTTLE:** I am not a bottle. I am material that is temporarily a bottle.

**THE BOTTLE:** One million of me are bought every minute. Twenty thousand every second. While you have been standing there.

**THE BOTTLE:** Two things happen to me next.

**THE BOTTLE:** One. Somebody keeps me separate, brings me here, and I am shredded, melted, and pressed. I become a bowl, a lamp, a tile. In forty years I become something else. The PET is unchanged.

**THE BOTTLE:** Two — and this is the average one — I go in a bin, or I end up floating in the water. The sun makes me brittle. I break into pieces. The pieces break into pieces.

**THE BOTTLE:** The bottle is finished either way. What happens after that is the only part anyone can still decide.

*CHRIS hands THE GUEST a scoop.*

**CHRIS:** Go on.

**THE GUEST:** I'll do it wrong.

**CHRIS:** Just scoop it slowly.

*THE GUEST feeds the extruder. Awkwardly at first. CHRIS adjusts nothing, lets them.*

*The machine changes pitch. A slow rope of red plastic begins to emerge.*

**THE GUEST:** Oh—

**CHRIS:** Yeah.

**THE GUEST:** It's warm.

**CHRIS:** Two hundred degrees warm. Don't.

*THE GUEST doesn't touch it. Watches.*

**THE GUEST:** I thought recycled plastic was — grey. Cheap-looking.

**KRIS:** It is, if you mix everything. All the colors together go brown, then grey. That's what happens when you process by the ton — you just do it all at once. Some brands add grey pigment on purpose, so it doesn't matter what goes in.

**CHRIS:** Which is fine. For a fence post.

**KRIS:** We sort by color. By hand. Which means if you want red, we sort of have to wait for red, and we have to painstakingly separate…

**THE GUEST:** How long does red take?

**KRIS:** That crate took somebody forty years.

**THE GUEST:** And if I want a specific color for the floor?

**CHRIS:** Then we're waiting on strangers to drink the right thing.

**KRIS:** Yes.

**CHRIS:** *(to THE GUEST)* Or I buy a sack of flake and you get your floor in November.

*A beat.*

**THE GUEST:** *(to KRIS)* And it's not stronger? Recycled?

**KRIS:** *(to THE GUEST)* It isn't harder. It isn't stronger. It's about the same, but if you get the polymers mixed it's worse. What it is, is honest. You can see what it's made of. You know where it came from because you were standing here. There is pretty much nothing else in your house you can say that about.

**CHRIS:** *(tightly)* That's a harder sell.

**KRIS:** It's a true one.

*THE MICROPLASTIC speaks.*

**THE MICROPLASTIC:** I am the second one.

**THE MICROPLASTIC:** Every gram held in a tile is a gram not in water, not in air, not in a body.

*KRIS transfers the extrusion into the sheet press. Closes it. Sets a weight.*

**KRIS:** Now we wait.

**THE GUEST:** How long?

**KRIS:** For this? a while…

*Nobody fills the silence. The press ticks as it cools. THE GUEST looks around the workshop — at the bins, the posters, the four solid jugs in the corner.*

**THE GUEST:** How long for my floor?

*KRIS doesn't answer yet. Opens the press. Lifts out a single tile — red, marbled, unmistakably made of what went in.*

*Hands it to THE GUEST. It's warm.*

**THE GUEST:** *(after a moment)* Ask me again.

**KRIS:** Ask you what?

**THE GUEST:** You said ask me again after I'd seen it. I'm asking.

*Lights shift.*

---

## Scene IV — The Offer

**KRIS:** Alright. Here's what I can offer you.

**KRIS:** You bring us your plastic. Yours. Out of your house. Rinsed, sorted by number. We hold it. When there's enough, we make your tiles out of that.

*A pause.*

**THE GUEST:** My own rubbish.

**KRIS:** Your own material.

**THE GUEST:** How long?

**KRIS:** I don't know.

**THE GUEST:** You must have some idea.

**KRIS:** I have no idea, because I don't know what you use, what your house throws away. Neither do you.

**THE GUEST:** I could guess.

**KRIS:** Then guess.

**THE GUEST:** *(a beat)* A bag a week?

**KRIS:** The average American throws out about three hundred pounds of plastic a year. Per person. People who count it are always wrong beforehand. Always low.

**THE GUEST:** So I'd count it.

**KRIS:** For a week. Everything. Then we'd both know.

**CHRIS:** And then it's still years.

**KRIS:** Who knows.

**CHRIS:** *(to THE GUEST)* Not two months. Years. My colleague is offering you homework and a waiting.

**THE GUEST:** Years of saving milk bottles and whatever else for a kitchen floor.

**CHRIS:** For a very good kitchen floor.

*THE GUEST is looking at the tile. Stops.*

**THE GUEST:** Hang on.

**KRIS:** Mm.

*KRIS waits.*

**THE GUEST:** People walk on floors. In shoes. With grit on them. Chairs get dragged. That's — that's abrasion, isn't it. I'd be laying down seventy square feet of it and then standing and walking and scraping on it every day…

**KRIS:** Yes.

**THE GUEST:** *(to CHRIS)* Were you going to tell me?

**CHRIS:** *(a beat)* I thought we would just seal it.

**KRIS:** The seal wears too.

**THE GUEST:** Wait, the tiles in the café aren't a floor. They're behind the coffee machine. On the wall.

**KRIS:** Nobody stands on a wall.

**THE GUEST:** So why did you let me talk about a floor for an hour?

**KRIS:** Well you asked about floor tiles; and because you wouldn't have taken my word for it. Now you figured it out on your own.

*A silence. CHRIS is the one who looks uncomfortable.*

**THE GUEST:** Splashback. Behind the sink. That's — what, ten square feet?

**KRIS:** About that.

**THE GUEST:** And how much time is ten square feet?

**KRIS:** Just count your plastic for a week. Then I'll tell you.

**THE GUEST:** What happens to it now? What I throw out.

**KRIS:** About five percent of it gets recycled. The rest is landfilled or burned.

**THE GUEST:** Five percent.

**KRIS:** Five.

**THE GUEST:** Ugh.

*A long beat.*

**CHRIS:** Can we get back to the other thing?

**CHRIS:** I take the café job. Forty tiles, broker flake, done soon, the café pays us. That money keeps the lights on and the machines running. And with the lights on — *(to THE GUEST)* — we do yours properly. Your material. However long it takes. We can afford to be slow about yours because of the proper café job.

*KRIS says nothing.*

**CHRIS:** You want it pure. I want it to exist. Those aren't the same but they're not opposites either.

**KRIS:** *(eventually)* The café tiles get labelled. What's in them, where it came from.

**CHRIS:** Fine.

**KRIS:** Every one.

**CHRIS:** I said fine.

*They both look at THE GUEST.*

**THE GUEST:** okay? so? we're doing it?

**KRIS:** That's your question, not ours.

*THE GUEST looks at the tile. At the bins. At the four solid jugs in the corner.*

**THE GUEST:** How do I count it?

*A beat. KRIS looks at CHRIS.*

**CHRIS:** *(a small concession)* There's a form. I'll print you a form. It helps with the codes and colors and such…

**THE GUEST:** And I bring it back in a week.

**KRIS:** You bring it back in a week.

**THE GUEST:** *(holding up the tile)* Can I keep this one?

**KRIS:** Sure, that one's yours. You made it.

*Lights shift.*

---

## Scene V — The Door Again

*THE GUEST at the door, holding the tile.*

**THE GUEST:** Okay, so what do I bring?

**KRIS:** All of it. Everything plastic that enters your house for a week.

**THE GUEST:** Even the stuff you can't use?

**KRIS:** Yeah, we still try to make sure it ends up in the right places.

*THE GUEST looks back at the room once — the bins, the posters, the four solid jugs in the corner…*

**THE GUEST:** *(reading the wall)* Look at your plastic.

*The guest leaves. The door closes.*

*KRIS goes back to the sorting table. CHRIS goes back to the shredder. Neither says anything for a moment.*

**CHRIS:** They won't come back.

**KRIS:** Some do.

*The shredder runs. Then stops.*

*The door opens. A NEW GUEST steps in.*

**NEW GUEST:** Sorry — are you open? I'm looking for tiles.

*CHRIS turns around immediately. KRIS does not.*

**CHRIS:** We're open.

*From somewhere in the room:*

**THE MICROPLASTIC:** I am a plastic fragment under five millimetres.

**THE MICROPLASTIC:** I am still here.

*THE NEW GUEST comes further in. Picks up a tile from the shelf. Turns it over. There's a number on the bottom.*

---

*END. BEGINNING.*

**PPP: People processing plastic.**
*We passionately produce new pieces thru boutique plastic processing. Our productive public performances, pedagogical praxis, and semi-practical products provide planetary peers the power to plan protocols for prioritizing a paradisiacal, pristine, unpolluted future.*

## Sources

**Bottle lifecycle** — days/weeks in distribution, minutes in use, centuries after: World Economic Forum summarising NOAA; Coda Plastics. Breakdown ~450 years: NOAA & Woods Hole Sea Grant marine debris estimates.

**One million bottles per minute; fewer than half collected; 7% return as bottles** — Euromonitor International, reported in *The Guardian*, 2017.

**Half a million fibres per polyester wash load** — Napper, I. E. & Thompson, R. C. (2016), "Release of synthetic microplastic plastic fibres from domestic washing machines," *Marine Pollution Bulletin* 112(1–2), 39–45. doi:10.1016/j.marpolbul.2016.09.025 *(6 kg load: polyester ~496,000 fibres; acrylic ~729,000.)*

**Polyester is 59% of global fibre production** — Textile Exchange, *Materials Market Report 2025* (2024 data). Synthetics overall: 69%. Textile-to-textile recycling remains under 1%; 98% of "recycled polyester" clothing comes from plastic bottles, not old clothes.

**Microplastics in the human body** — Blood: Leslie et al. (2022), *Environment International*, doi:10.1016/j.envint.2022.107199. Placenta: Ragusa et al. (2021), *Environment International*, doi:10.1016/j.envint.2020.106274. Breast milk: Ragusa et al. (2022), *Polymers* 14(13):2700, doi:10.3390/polym14132700. Lungs: Jenner et al. (2022), *Science of the Total Environment*, doi:10.1016/j.scitotenv.2022.154907. Placental barrier crossing: Frontiers in Endocrinology (2024), doi:10.3389/fendo.2024.1481014.

**~300 lb of plastic discarded per American per year** — Greenpeace USA (2022), from 51 million tons discarded in 2021.

**~5% US plastic recycling rate** — Beyond Plastics & The Last Beach Cleanup (2022); EPA reported 8.7% for 2018. Approximately 86% landfilled, 9% incinerated.

**Vinyl plank flooring is PVC (resin code 3)** — post-consumer PVC recycling is negligible; LVP is the leading kitchen flooring choice in the US.

**Flooring comparison prices** — 2026 US material costs: LVP $2–5/sq ft, ceramic tile $1–5, solid red oak $3–8. Installed roughly doubles.

**Resin Identification Codes** — ASTM International D7611. An RIC identifies polymer type; it is not a recyclability claim.

**Single-stream sorting losses** — small-format items including loose closures fall through disc screens and report to residue. Standard MRF engineering.

**Tile arithmetic** — HDPE cap ≈ 2.3 g; 90 caps ≈ 207 g ≈ one 6" × 6" tile at ~10 mm. 4 tiles/sq ft → ~360 caps/sq ft. 70 sq ft ≈ 25,000 caps ≈ 12 years at 40/week. 10 sq ft ≈ 3,600 caps ≈ under 2 years.

**Milk jug calculation** — 1 US gallon ≈ 3.785 L; HDPE density ≈ 0.95 g/cm³ → solid mass ≈ 3.6 kg (7.9 lb). Empty jug ≈ 65 g → ~55 jugs per solid form, ~220 for four. A full gallon of milk ≈ 3.9 kg (8.6 lb).
