# People Processing Plastic
### A play in five scenes

---

*People wander in.*
*Conversations happen.*
*Someone leaves changed.*
*The door opens again.*

---

## A Note on Production

Perform this in a working plastic workshop. The shredder, the extruder, the press are not props — they run, they're loud, and the room smells of warm plastic. The tile made during the performance is real. At the end it is given away.

The Bottle and the Microplastic are documentary voices. They state facts about their own existence and stop. They're triggered by words in the human conversation — someone says *microplastics*, the Microplastic speaks — and they never introduce themselves. Don't costume them. They are not characters. They are the material with a microphone.

All roles are written for any performer. No character has a gender.

We are not anti-plastic. We're against wasting a permanent material on a few minutes of use.

---

## Cast

**KRIS** — Founded the workshop. Believes the objects are an argument, and that where the material came from is most of the argument. Would rather send someone away with an idea than a sale.

**CHRIS** — Believes the objects are the argument, and an argument nobody buys isn't one. Wants to deliver, however they can.

**THE GUEST** — Came to buy tiles for a kitchen floor. Four square meters.

**THE NEW GUEST** — Arrives at the end.

**THE BOTTLE** · **THE MICROPLASTIC** — Voices.

## Scene I — The Order

*A Precious Plastic workshop. Shredder, extruder warming up, sorting table. Bins labeled HDPE, PET, PP. A shelf of finished tiles — multicolored, marbled. Four dense grey-blue forms in the corner, the shape of milk jugs, a handle faintly visible in each. On the walls: hand-made posters, resin codes, a circle diagram. Near the door: PPP — People Processing Plastic.*

*KRIS at the sorting table. CHRIS at the shredder, feeding in bottle caps — red, blue, white.*

**THE BOTTLE:** Days in a truck. Weeks on a shelf.

**THE BOTTLE:** Minutes in a hand.

**THE BOTTLE:** Centuries after that.

**THE BOTTLE:** More time was spent moving me to you than you spent using me.

**THE BOTTLE:** That is not an accident. That is the design.

**THE BOTTLE:** I am PET. Fewer than half of us are collected. Of the ones collected, seven percent come back as bottles.

**THE BOTTLE:** The rest become something lesser. Or nothing.

*The shredder runs. CHRIS shuts it off. Silence, suddenly.*

**CHRIS:** The café wants forty more.

**KRIS:** We said twenty.

**CHRIS:** They liked the twenty.

**KRIS:** *(still sorting)* We're not a tile company.

**CHRIS:** We're not anything. That's the problem.

**KRIS:** We're a demonstration.

**CHRIS:** A demonstration nobody's paying for.

*KRIS holds up a piece of plastic. Reads the number on the bottom. Sets it in a bin.*

**KRIS:** That bag's mixed. Sort it before it goes in.

**CHRIS:** It's mostly HDPE.

**KRIS:** The number on the bottom doesn't say "mostly."

*CHRIS pulls the bag. Sorts it. This has happened before.*

**CHRIS:** *(holding up a red cap)* Somebody dropped off a crate of these. All the same red. Forty years of one family's ketchup.

**KRIS:** *(a beat, genuinely)* Huh.

**CHRIS:** That's a tabletop. That's a good tabletop.

**KRIS:** That's a hundred and fifty conversations.

**CHRIS:** It can be both.

**KRIS:** It's never been both.

*The door opens. THE GUEST steps in.*

**THE GUEST:** Sorry — are you open? I'm looking for tiles.

*CHRIS turns around immediately. KRIS does not.*

**CHRIS:** We're open.

**THE GUEST:** I saw them at a café on Wharf Street. Behind the espresso machine — the whole wall of them. I asked where they came from and somebody wrote this address on a napkin.

**CHRIS:** That's us.

**THE GUEST:** I want to do my kitchen floor.

*A pause. KRIS stops sorting.*

**KRIS:** How much floor?

**THE GUEST:** About four square meters.

*KRIS and CHRIS look at each other. Something passes between them that THE GUEST can't read.*

**CHRIS:** Four square meters.

**THE GUEST:** Is that a lot?

**CHRIS:** *(picking up a handful of flakes)* Depends what you mean by a lot.

*THE GUEST comes further in. Looks at the shelf. Picks up a tile. Turns it over. There's a number molded into the bottom.*

**THE GUEST:** What's the number?

**CHRIS:** Resin code. Every piece of plastic has one. Tells you what it's made of. That's a two. HDPE.

**THE GUEST:** And the bins?

**CHRIS:** One number per bin. Keep them separate, you keep the value. Mix them—

**KRIS:** Landfill.

**THE GUEST:** So this was — what, bottles?

**CHRIS:** Caps. Different colours because different brands. Same material.

**THE GUEST:** Out of trash.

*A small pause. They've heard this word.*

**CHRIS:** Waste. Trash is a decision. Waste is material between purposes.

**THE GUEST:** How many caps in one tile?

**CHRIS:** Depends on thickness. That one, maybe ninety.

**THE GUEST:** And four square meters?

*CHRIS doesn't answer immediately. KRIS answers instead.*

**KRIS:** About sixteen thousand caps.

*THE GUEST looks at the bins. Then at the tile in their hand.*

**THE GUEST:** How many do you have?

**KRIS:** Today? Maybe two thousand.

**THE GUEST:** So how long?

**KRIS:** Depends what people bring us.

**THE GUEST:** That's not an answer.

**KRIS:** No. It isn't.

*THE GUEST moves further in, uncertain now. Stops at the corner — four dense forms, grey-blue, marbled. Crouches. Tries to lift one. It barely moves.*

**THE GUEST:** What are these?

**CHRIS:** Milk jugs.

**THE GUEST:** It's solid.

**CHRIS:** Yeah.

**THE GUEST:** How many?

**CHRIS:** That one's fifty-five. Those four together, two hundred and twenty. People kept bringing them in. Couldn't stop once they started.

**THE GUEST:** How much does it weigh?

**CHRIS:** About the same as a full one.

*THE GUEST stands. Looks at it differently.*

**THE GUEST:** So can you do my floor or not?

*KRIS and CHRIS look at each other again.*

**CHRIS:** Yes.

**KRIS:** No.

*Lights shift.*

## Scene II — The Misclassification

*Nobody moves. THE GUEST looks between them.*

**THE GUEST:** Is that a joke?

**CHRIS:** No.

**KRIS:** We disagree about what this place is.

**CHRIS:** *(to KRIS)* Sixteen thousand caps at forty a week is—

**KRIS:** Eight years.

**CHRIS:** If we put the word out it's not eight years. The ketchup crate came in off one flyer.

**KRIS:** And then we're a floor company with a waiting list.

**CHRIS:** We'd be a company. That's not the insult you think it is.

*THE GUEST, awkward, still holding the tile.*

**THE GUEST:** I could just — I mean, I could buy tiles.

**KRIS:** You could. Twelve pounds a square meter. Delivered Thursday.

**THE GUEST:** But I don't want those.

**CHRIS:** *(to KRIS, pointed)* They don't want those.

*KRIS goes back to sorting. Doesn't answer.*

**THE GUEST:** Can I ask something? I recycle. Properly. Everything gets rinsed. Why isn't that enough?

*KRIS stops sorting.*

**KRIS:** What bin do the caps go in?

**THE GUEST:** Same one. Everything goes in the same one.

**KRIS:** Single stream. One truck, one bin, sorted later by machine.

**THE GUEST:** Right.

**KRIS:** *(holding up a red cap)* This doesn't survive that. It's too small. At the facility it drops through the screens with the glass fines and the grit and goes out as residue. Straight to landfill.

*A beat.*

**THE GUEST:** The caps.

**CHRIS:** All of them. Every cap you've ever rinsed.

**KRIS:** And the glass shatters on the way in — it has to, that's how the line separates it — so now there's broken glass in everything. In the paper bales. In the plastic. Abrasive contamination drives maintenance costs up sharply. Most of what comes off the end of a single-stream line as residue is glass.

**THE GUEST:** Then why do it that way?

**KRIS:** Because one bin is cheaper to collect than three. More people participate, the truck goes to one place, labour costs drop. It's an excellent collection system.

**THE GUEST:** But not a recovery system.

**KRIS:** It was optimised for the truck. Not for the material.

*THE GUEST sets down the tile. Picks up a bottle from the nearest bin, turns it over, checks the number. Neither of them comments, but both notice.*

**THE GUEST:** And the stuff that doesn't get sorted?

**CHRIS:** It gets small.

**THE GUEST:** Small.

**KRIS:** Plastic doesn't rot. It breaks. Into pieces too small to sort, too small to collect, too small to see. Still plastic. Still permanent.

*A beat.*

**THE MICROPLASTIC:** I am a plastic fragment under five millimetres.

**THE MICROPLASTIC:** I am being made right now. By the shirt on your back, if it's polyester. Polyester is PET. Resin code one. The same polymer as a water bottle.

**THE MICROPLASTIC:** One laundry load of synthetic clothes releases up to seven hundred thousand fibres. Sixty percent of clothing fibre is plastic.

**THE MICROPLASTIC:** I am also every carrier bag that went brittle in the sun. Every length of polypropylene rope that frayed in the water. Every container left outside for a season.

**THE MICROPLASTIC:** I am not only what happens to plastic when it's thrown away. I am what happens while it's still being used.

**THE MICROPLASTIC:** I have been found in human blood. In lungs. In placentas. In breast milk. Particles under ten micrometres cross the placental barrier. I was in the body before the body was born.

**THE MICROPLASTIC:** I am associated with oxidative stress, inflammation, neurotoxic effects, reproductive harm. The evidence is still building. The particles are already there.

*THE GUEST is very still.*

**THE GUEST:** Did you hear that?

**CHRIS:** *(not looking up)* Extruder does that warming up.

*THE GUEST doesn't believe them. Lets it go.*

**THE GUEST:** So a floor made of this — that's plastic that isn't doing that.

**KRIS:** *(carefully)* Yes.

**CHRIS:** *(to KRIS)* Say the rest of it.

**KRIS:** Four square meters of tile is sixteen thousand caps that stay solid instead of shedding. That's not nothing.

**CHRIS:** It's the entire argument.

**KRIS:** It's half the argument.

**CHRIS:** *(to THE GUEST)* Four square meters. I can do it. Two months.

**KRIS:** With what?

**CHRIS:** There's a broker in Feltham selling washed HDPE flake by the sack. Post-industrial, clean, single polymer, better than anything that walks through that door. Eighty pence a kilo.

**KRIS:** So we'd be buying material.

**CHRIS:** We'd be *delivering*. Somebody wants a floor made out of recovered plastic instead of a floor made out of new plastic. That's the win. That's the entire thing we say we're for. And we're going to lose it because the caps didn't arrive with the right story attached?

**KRIS:** The story is the product.

**CHRIS:** The product is the product. People don't want a demonstration in their kitchen. They want a floor.

*Silence.*

**THE GUEST:** *(carefully)* Would it be the same?

**CHRIS:** Chemically? Identical.

**THE GUEST:** That's not what I asked.

*Neither of them answers.*

**KRIS:** *(after a moment)* Not yet.

**THE GUEST:** Not yet what?

**KRIS:** You should see how it's made first. Then ask me again.

*Lights shift.*

## Scene III — The Making

*KRIS drags a crate to the shredder. Bottle caps. All the same red.*

**CHRIS:** That's the ketchup.

**KRIS:** I know.

**CHRIS:** That's the tabletop.

**KRIS:** It's four hundred caps. It's not a tabletop, it's a story about a tabletop.

*KRIS switches on the shredder. It's loud — genuinely loud. Conversation stops. Caps go in whole and come out as flake. It takes as long as it takes.*

*KRIS shuts it off. Holds up a handful. Red, uniform, catching the light.*

**KRIS:** *(to THE GUEST)* That's forty years of one family's condiments. Took ninety seconds.

**THE GUEST:** Can I ask something that might be stupid?

**KRIS:** Probably not.

**THE GUEST:** Why plastic at all? Why not just stop?

**CHRIS:** New plastic, yes. Stop tomorrow.

**KRIS:** But we've already made it. That's the part people skip. We have made more plastic than we will ever need. It's already here — in the ground, in the sea, in that crate, blowing down the street outside.

**THE GUEST:** So there's enough.

**KRIS:** There's more than enough. And it lasts forever. We built a material that lasts forever and then we used it once.

*They tip the flake into a hopper.*

**KRIS:** So the job isn't to stop using plastic. It's to stop making it out of oil, and take seriously the permanent material we already made.

**THE GUEST:** Meaning what, practically?

**KRIS:** Two things. Put it into objects solid enough and good enough that it stays put — a floor, a wall, a bench — so it isn't shedding into anything. And when that object's finished, take it back and make the next one. Forever. Without ever pulling up more oil.

*A beat. The extruder is up to temperature.*

**THE BOTTLE:** I am not a bottle. I am material that is temporarily a bottle.

**THE BOTTLE:** One million of me are bought every minute. Twenty thousand every second. While you have been standing there.

**THE BOTTLE:** Two things happen to me next.

**THE BOTTLE:** One. Somebody keeps me separate, brings me here, and I am shredded, melted, and pressed. I become something solid. In forty years I become something else. The PET is unchanged. It was always unchanged.

**THE BOTTLE:** Two — and this is the average one — I go in a bin, or I go in the water. The sun makes me brittle. I break into pieces. The pieces break into pieces. Four hundred and fifty years of that.

**THE BOTTLE:** The bottle is finished either way. What happens after that is the only part anyone can still decide.

*CHRIS hands THE GUEST a scoop.*

**CHRIS:** Go on.

**THE GUEST:** I'll do it wrong.

**CHRIS:** You'll do it slowly.

*THE GUEST feeds the extruder. Awkwardly at first. CHRIS adjusts nothing, lets them.*

*The machine changes pitch. A slow rope of red plastic begins to emerge.*

**THE GUEST:** Oh—

**CHRIS:** Yeah.

**THE GUEST:** It's warm.

**CHRIS:** Two hundred degrees warm. Don't.

*THE GUEST doesn't touch it. Watches.*

**THE GUEST:** I thought recycled plastic was — grey. Cheap-looking.

**KRIS:** It is, if you mix everything. All the colours together go brown, then grey. That's what happens when you process by the tonne — nobody can sort by colour at that speed, so everything comes out the colour of everything.

**CHRIS:** Which is fine. For a fence post.

**KRIS:** We sort by colour. By hand. Which means if you want red, we wait for red.

**THE GUEST:** How long does red take?

**KRIS:** That crate took somebody forty years.

**THE GUEST:** And if I want a specific colour for the floor?

**CHRIS:** Then we're waiting on strangers to drink the right thing.

**KRIS:** Yes.

**CHRIS:** *(to THE GUEST)* Or I buy a sack of flake and you get your floor in November.

**THE GUEST:** What colour is the sack?

*A beat.*

**CHRIS:** Whatever colour they've got.

*Silence. CHRIS goes back to the machine.*

**THE GUEST:** *(to KRIS)* And it's not stronger? Recycled?

**CHRIS:** It's harder than virgin, actually.

**KRIS:** It isn't.

*A pause. CHRIS looks at KRIS.*

**KRIS:** *(to THE GUEST)* It isn't harder. It isn't stronger. It's about the same, and if you get the polymers mixed it's worse. What it is, is honest. You can see what it's made of. You know where it came from because you were standing here. There is nothing else in your house you can say that about.

**CHRIS:** *(tightly)* That's a harder sell.

**KRIS:** It's a true one.

*THE MICROPLASTIC speaks.*

**THE MICROPLASTIC:** I am the second one.

**THE MICROPLASTIC:** Every gram held in a floor is a gram not in water, not in air, not in a body.

*KRIS transfers the extrusion into the sheet press. Closes it. Sets a weight.*

**KRIS:** Now we wait.

**THE GUEST:** How long?

**KRIS:** For this? Six minutes.

*Nobody fills the silence. The press ticks as it cools. THE GUEST looks around the workshop — at the bins, the posters, the four solid jugs in the corner.*

**THE GUEST:** How long for a floor?

**KRIS:** Which floor?

**THE GUEST:** Mine.

*KRIS doesn't answer yet. Opens the press. Lifts out a single tile — red, marbled, unmistakably made of what went in.*

*Hands it to THE GUEST. It's warm.*

**THE GUEST:** *(after a moment)* Ask me again.

**KRIS:** Ask you what?

**THE GUEST:** You said ask me again after I'd seen it. I'm asking.

*Lights shift.*


## Scene IV — The Offer

**KRIS:** Alright. Here's what I can offer you.

**KRIS:** You bring us your plastic. Yours. Out of your house. Sorted, rinsed, by number. We hold it. When there's enough, we make it out of that.

*A pause.*

**THE GUEST:** My own rubbish.

**KRIS:** Your own material.

**THE GUEST:** How long?

**KRIS:** I don't know.

**THE GUEST:** You must have some idea.

**KRIS:** I have no idea, because I don't know what your house throws away. Neither do you.

**THE GUEST:** I could guess.

**KRIS:** Guess.

**THE GUEST:** *(a beat)* A bag a week?

**KRIS:** The average household in this country throws away sixty pieces of plastic packaging a week. Three thousand a year. People who count it are always wrong beforehand. Always low.

**THE GUEST:** So I'd count it.

**KRIS:** For a week. Everything. Then we'd both know.

**CHRIS:** And then it's still years.

**KRIS:** Probably.

**CHRIS:** *(to THE GUEST)* Not two months. Years. They're offering you homework and a wait.

**THE GUEST:** Years of saving milk bottles for a kitchen floor.

**CHRIS:** For a very good kitchen floor.

*THE GUEST is looking at the tile. Stops.*

**THE GUEST:** Hang on.

**KRIS:** Mm.

**THE GUEST:** Shoes.

*KRIS waits.*

**THE GUEST:** People walk on floors. In shoes. With grit on them. Chairs get dragged. That's — that's abrasion, isn't it. That's the thing you've been describing all afternoon. I'd be laying down four square meters of it and then standing on it every day for twenty years.

**KRIS:** Yes.

**THE GUEST:** *(to CHRIS)* Were you going to tell me?

**CHRIS:** *(a beat)* I was going to seal it.

**KRIS:** The seal wears too.

**THE GUEST:** The ones in the café aren't a floor. They're behind the coffee machine. On the wall.

**KRIS:** Nobody stands on a wall.

**THE GUEST:** So why did you let me talk about a floor for an hour?

**KRIS:** Because you'd have taken my word for it. Now you won't have to.

*A silence. CHRIS is the one who looks uncomfortable.*

**THE GUEST:** Splashback. Behind the sink. That's — what, a square meter?

**KRIS:** About that.

**THE GUEST:** And how long is a square meter?

**KRIS:** Count your week. Then I'll tell you.

**THE GUEST:** What happens to it now? What I throw out.

**KRIS:** Nationally? Fifty-nine percent gets burnt. Sixteen percent recycled. The rest exported or landfilled.

**THE GUEST:** Burnt.

**KRIS:** Burnt.

**THE GUEST:** I did not know that.

**KRIS:** No.

*A long beat.*

**CHRIS:** Can I say the other thing.

**KRIS:** You're going to.

**CHRIS:** I take the café job. Forty tiles, broker flake, done by March, they pay us. That money keeps the lights on and the machines running. And with the lights on — *(to THE GUEST)* — we do yours properly. Your material. However long it takes. We can afford to be slow about yours because we were fast about theirs.

*KRIS says nothing.*

**CHRIS:** You want it pure. I want it to exist. Those aren't the same fight but they're not opposite ones either.

**KRIS:** *(eventually)* The café tiles get labelled. What's in them, where it came from.

**CHRIS:** Fine.

**KRIS:** Every one.

**CHRIS:** I said fine.

*They both look at THE GUEST.*

**THE GUEST:** So which am I?

**KRIS:** That's your question, not ours.

*THE GUEST looks at the tile. At the bins. At the four solid jugs in the corner.*

**THE GUEST:** How do I count it?

*A beat. KRIS looks at CHRIS.*

**CHRIS:** *(a small concession)* There's a form. I'll print you a form.

**THE GUEST:** And I bring it back in a week.

**KRIS:** You bring it back in a week.

**THE GUEST:** *(holding up the tile)* Can I keep this one?

**KRIS:** That one's yours. You made it.

*Lights shift.*

## Scene V — The Door Again

*THE GUEST at the door, holding the tile.*

**THE GUEST:** What do I bring?

**KRIS:** All of it. Everything plastic that leaves your house for a week.

**THE GUEST:** Even the stuff you can't use?

**KRIS:** Especially that.

*THE GUEST looks back at the room once — the bins, the posters, the four solid jugs in the corner. Two hundred and twenty.*

**THE GUEST:** *(reading the wall)* Look at your plastic.

*They leave. The door closes.*

*KRIS goes back to the sorting table. CHRIS goes back to the shredder. Neither says anything for a moment.*

**CHRIS:** They won't come back.

**KRIS:** Some do.

**CHRIS:** *(feeding the shredder)* Print the form anyway?

**KRIS:** Print the form.

*The shredder runs. Then stops.*

*The door opens. A NEW GUEST steps in.*

**NEW GUEST:** Sorry — are you open? I'm looking for tiles.

*CHRIS turns around immediately. KRIS does not.*

**CHRIS:** We're open.

*From somewhere in the room:*

**THE MICROPLASTIC:** I am a plastic fragment under five millimetres.

**THE MICROPLASTIC:** I am still here.

*THE NEW GUEST comes further in. Picks up a tile from the shelf. Turns it over. There's a number on the bottom.*

*The machines keep running.*

*END. BEGINNING.*

## Appendix: The PPP Mission Statement

*Posted near the door of the workshop. Read, if at all, in passing.*

**PPP: People processing plastic.**
*We passionately produce new pieces thru boutique plastic processing. Our productive public performances, pedagogical praxis, and semi-practical products provide planetary peers the power to plan protocols for prioritizing a paradisiacal, pristine, unpolluted future.*

## Sources

Every figure spoken in this play is checked. Full annotations in the accompanying fact-check.

**Bottle lifecycle** — Days/weeks in distribution, minutes in use, centuries after: Coda Plastics; World Economic Forum summarising NOAA. Breakdown time ~450 years: NOAA & Woods Hole Sea Grant marine debris estimates.

**One million bottles per minute; fewer than half collected; 7% return as bottles** — Euromonitor International global packaging trends, reported in *The Guardian*, 2017.

**700,000 fibres per wash load** — Napper, I. E. & Thompson, R. C. (2016), "Release of synthetic microplastic plastic fibres from domestic washing machines," *Marine Pollution Bulletin* 112(1–2), 39–45. doi:10.1016/j.marpolbul.2016.09.025

**~60% of clothing fibre is synthetic** — Textile Exchange, Preferred Fiber & Materials Market Report.

**Microplastics in human blood** — Leslie et al. (2022), *Environment International*. doi:10.1016/j.envint.2022.107199
**In placenta** — Ragusa et al. (2021), *Environment International*. doi:10.1016/j.envint.2020.106274
**In breast milk** — Ragusa et al. (2022), *Polymers* 14(13):2700. doi:10.3390/polym14132700
**In lungs** — Jenner et al. (2022), *Science of the Total Environment*. doi:10.1016/j.scitotenv.2022.154907
**Placental barrier crossing, health associations** — Frontiers in Endocrinology (2024), doi:10.3389/fendo.2024.1481014; systematic review, PMC11186737.

**60 pieces of plastic packaging per household per week; 59% incinerated, 16% recycled** — The Big Plastic Count (Greenpeace UK & Everyday Plastic), 2024. thebigplasticcount.com

**~15% of plastic packaging recycled, ~40% mismanaged globally** — Pew Charitable Trusts & SYSTEMIQ (2020), *Breaking the Plastic Wave*.
**~9% of all plastic ever produced has been recycled** — OECD (2022), *Global Plastics Outlook*.

**Resin Identification Codes** — ASTM International D7611. Note: an RIC identifies polymer type. It is not a recyclability claim.

**Single-stream sorting losses** — Small-format items including loose closures fall through disc screens and report to residue. Standard MRF engineering; see industry literature on 2-inch-minus fines and glass contamination.

**Milk jug calculation** — 1 US gallon interior ≈ 3.785 L; HDPE density ≈ 0.95 g/cm³ → solid mass ≈ 3.6 kg (7.9 lb). Empty jug ≈ 65 g → ~55 jugs per solid form; ~220 for four. A full gallon of milk ≈ 3.9 kg (8.6 lb).
