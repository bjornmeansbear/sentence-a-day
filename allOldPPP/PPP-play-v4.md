## A Note on Production

This play is designed to be performed in a Precious Plastic workshop — or any space where plastic is visibly being processed. The machines — shredder, extruder, mold — are not props. They work. The sounds are real. The smell of warm plastic is real. The object made during the performance is real, and at the end, it is given away.

On the walls: hand-made materials explaining the types of plastic, their resin codes, what they can become. Nothing glossy. Nothing corporate. The kind of thing someone made themselves, with care, because they wanted people to understand. A circle diagram showing the loop. The words LOOK AT YOUR PLASTIC somewhere visible. A sign near the door: PPP: People Processing Plastic.

The plastic characters — The Bottle, The Pellet, The Microplastic, The Sheet — are documentary voices. They provide factual testimony about their own existence. They are triggered by words spoken in the human conversation — someone says "microplastics" and the Microplastic speaks; someone holds up a bottle and the Bottle speaks. They do not introduce themselves. The audience learns who they are from what they say.

They should not be costumed. They are not characters in a play. They are the material itself, given a microphone.

We are not anti-plastic. We are anti-misuse of plastic. This is not about celebrating plastic but about challenging its wasteful use and demonstrating that even the most problematic materials can have a meaningful place in a circular, regenerative system.

The play should feel like a conversation that got interesting. Not a lecture. Never a lecture.

## Cast of Characters

**KRIS** — Workshop founder. Has been doing this for years. Unhurried. Thinks in systems but speaks in specifics.

**CHRIS** — Kris's collaborator. Engineer by training. Finds beauty in process. Knows the temperature at which everything melts.

**THE GUEST** — Wandered in off the street. Curious. Slightly guilty. Doesn't know why they stopped.

**THE NEW GUEST** — Arrives at the end. Identical entrance. The circle turning.

**THE BOTTLE** — A voice. Documents the lifecycle of a single-use plastic object. Factual. Specific. The gap between what plastic is and how we use it, stated plainly.

**THE PELLET** — A voice. The raw material. Documents its own origin, its potential, its fate when it doesn't make it into the technical cycle.

**THE MICROPLASTIC** — A voice. A documentary witness. Speaks in facts about where it is, how it got there, and what it would take to bring it back.

**THE SHEET** — A voice. The recovered material. Documents what plastic can become when the technical cycle works.

## Scene I — The Order

*A Precious Plastic workshop. Shredder, extruder warming up, sorting table. Bins labeled HDPE, PET, PP. A shelf of finished tiles — multicolored, marbled. Four dense grey-blue forms in the corner, the shape of milk jugs, a handle faintly visible in each. On the walls: hand-made posters, resin codes, a circle diagram. Near the door: PPP — People Processing Plastic.*

*KRIS at the sorting table. CHRIS at the shredder, feeding in bottle caps — red, blue, white.*

**THE BOTTLE:** I am made from material that can last forever.

**THE BOTTLE:** You know how long the average bottle is actually used for?

*A beat.*

**THE BOTTLE:** Eleven minutes.

**THE BOTTLE:** Then I was classified as waste. Sent away. Nobody asked where away was.

**THE BOTTLE:** Away was a sorting facility in another country. I arrived mixed with materials I wasn't compatible with. I left as landfill.

*The shredder runs. CHRIS shuts it off. Silence, suddenly.*

**CHRIS:** The café wants forty more.

**KRIS:** We said twenty.

**CHRIS:** They liked the twenty.

**KRIS:** *(still sorting)* We're not a tile company.

**CHRIS:** We're not anything. That's the problem.

**KRIS:** We're a demonstration.

**CHRIS:** A demonstration nobody's paying for.

*KRIS holds up a piece of plastic. Reads the number on the bottom. Sets it in a bin.*

**KRIS:** That bag's mixed. Sort it before it goes in.

**CHRIS:** It's mostly HDPE.

**KRIS:** The number on the bottom doesn't say "mostly."

*CHRIS pulls the bag. Sorts it. This has happened before.*

**CHRIS:** *(holding up a red cap)* Somebody dropped off a crate of these. All the same red. Forty years of one family's ketchup.

**KRIS:** *(a beat, genuinely)* Huh.

**CHRIS:** That's a tabletop. That's a good tabletop.

**KRIS:** That's a hundred and fifty conversations.

**CHRIS:** It can be both.

**KRIS:** It's never been both.

*The door opens. THE GUEST steps in.*

**THE GUEST:** Sorry — are you open? I'm looking for tiles.

*CHRIS turns around immediately. KRIS does not.*

**CHRIS:** We're open.

**THE GUEST:** I saw one at a café on Wharf Street. On the counter. I asked where it came from and they wrote this address on a napkin.

**CHRIS:** That's us.

**THE GUEST:** I want to do my kitchen floor.

*A pause. KRIS stops sorting.*

**KRIS:** How much floor?

**THE GUEST:** About four square meters.

*KRIS and CHRIS look at each other. Something passes between them that THE GUEST can't read.*

**CHRIS:** Four square meters.

**THE GUEST:** Is that a lot?

**CHRIS:** *(picking up a handful of flakes)* Depends what you mean by a lot.

*THE GUEST comes further in. Looks at the shelf. Picks up a tile. Turns it over. There's a number molded into the bottom.*

**THE GUEST:** What's the number?

**CHRIS:** Resin code. Every piece of plastic has one. Tells you what it's made of. That's a two. HDPE.

**THE GUEST:** And the bins?

**CHRIS:** One number per bin. Keep them separate, you keep the value. Mix them—

**KRIS:** Landfill.

**THE GUEST:** So this was — what, bottles?

**CHRIS:** Caps. Different colours because different brands. Same material.

**THE GUEST:** Out of trash.

*A small pause. They've heard this word.*

**CHRIS:** Waste. Trash is a decision. Waste is material between purposes.

**THE GUEST:** How many caps in one tile?

**CHRIS:** Depends on thickness. That one, maybe ninety.

**THE GUEST:** And four square meters?

*CHRIS doesn't answer immediately. KRIS answers instead.*

**KRIS:** About sixteen thousand caps.

*THE GUEST looks at the bins. Then at the tile in their hand.*

**THE GUEST:** How many do you have?

**KRIS:** Today? Maybe two thousand.

**THE GUEST:** So how long?

**KRIS:** Depends what people bring us.

**THE GUEST:** That's not an answer.

**KRIS:** No. It isn't.

*THE GUEST moves further in, uncertain now. Stops at the corner — four dense forms, grey-blue, marbled. Crouches. Tries to lift one. It barely moves.*

**THE GUEST:** What are these?

**CHRIS:** Milk jugs.

**THE GUEST:** It's solid.

**CHRIS:** Yeah.

**THE GUEST:** How many?

**CHRIS:** That one's fifty-five. Those four together, two hundred and twenty. People kept bringing them in. Couldn't stop once they started.

**THE GUEST:** How much does it weigh?

**CHRIS:** About the same as a full one.

*THE GUEST stands. Looks at it differently.*

**THE GUEST:** So can you do my floor or not?

*KRIS and CHRIS look at each other again.*

**CHRIS:** Yes.

**KRIS:** No.

*Lights shift.*

## Scene II — The Misclassification

*Nobody moves. THE GUEST looks between them.*

**THE GUEST:** Is that a joke?

**CHRIS:** No.

**KRIS:** We disagree about what this place is.

**CHRIS:** *(to KRIS)* Sixteen thousand caps at forty a week is—

**KRIS:** Eight years.

**CHRIS:** If we put the word out it's not eight years. The ketchup crate came in off one flyer.

**KRIS:** And then we're a floor company with a waiting list.

**CHRIS:** We'd be a company. That's not the insult you think it is.

*THE GUEST, awkward, still holding the tile.*

**THE GUEST:** I could just — I mean, I could buy tiles.

**KRIS:** You could. Twelve pounds a square meter. Delivered Thursday.

**THE GUEST:** But I don't want those.

**CHRIS:** *(to KRIS, pointed)* They don't want those.

*KRIS goes back to sorting. Doesn't answer.*

**THE GUEST:** Can I ask something? I recycle. Properly. Everything gets rinsed. Why isn't that enough?

*KRIS stops sorting.*

**KRIS:** What bin do the caps go in?

**THE GUEST:** Same one. Everything goes in the same one.

**KRIS:** Single stream. One truck, one bin, sorted later by machine.

**THE GUEST:** Right.

**KRIS:** *(holding up a red cap)* This doesn't survive that. It's too small. At the facility it drops through the screens with the glass fines and the grit and goes out as residue. Straight to landfill.

*A beat.*

**THE GUEST:** The caps.

**CHRIS:** All of them. Every cap you've ever rinsed.

**KRIS:** And the glass shatters on the way in — it has to, that's how the line separates it — so now there's broken glass in everything. In the paper bales. In the plastic. Abrasive contamination has put maintenance costs up over three hundred percent at some facilities. Most of what comes off the end of a single-stream line as residue is glass.

**THE GUEST:** Then why do it that way?

**KRIS:** Because one bin is cheaper to collect than three. More people participate, the truck goes to one place, labour costs drop. It's an excellent collection system.

**THE GUEST:** But not a recovery system.

**KRIS:** It was optimised for the truck. Not for the material.

*THE GUEST sets down the tile. Picks up a bottle from the nearest bin, turns it over, checks the number. Neither of them comments, but both notice.*

**THE GUEST:** And the stuff that doesn't get sorted?

**CHRIS:** It gets small.

**THE GUEST:** Small.

**KRIS:** Plastic doesn't rot. It breaks. Into pieces too small to sort, too small to collect, too small to see. Still plastic. Still permanent.

*A beat.*

**THE MICROPLASTIC:** I am a plastic fragment under five millimetres.

**THE MICROPLASTIC:** I am being made right now. By the shirt on your back, if it's polyester. Polyester is PET. Resin code one. The same polymer as a water bottle.

**THE MICROPLASTIC:** One laundry load of synthetic clothes releases up to seven hundred thousand fibres. Sixty percent of clothing fibre is plastic.

**THE MICROPLASTIC:** I am also every carrier bag that went brittle in the sun. Every length of polypropylene rope that frayed in the water. Every container left outside for a season.

**THE MICROPLASTIC:** I am not only what happens to plastic when it's thrown away. I am what happens while it's still being used.

**THE MICROPLASTIC:** I have been found in human blood. In lungs. In placentas. In breast milk. Particles under ten micrometres cross the placental barrier. I was in the body before the body was born.

**THE MICROPLASTIC:** I am associated with oxidative stress, inflammation, neurotoxic effects, reproductive harm. The evidence is still building. The particles are already there.

*THE GUEST is very still.*

**THE GUEST:** Did you hear that?

**CHRIS:** *(not looking up)* Extruder does that warming up.

*THE GUEST doesn't believe them. Lets it go.*

**THE GUEST:** So a floor made of this — that's plastic that isn't doing that.

**KRIS:** *(carefully)* Yes.

**CHRIS:** *(to KRIS)* Say the rest of it.

**KRIS:** Four square meters of tile is sixteen thousand caps that stay solid instead of shedding. That's not nothing.

**CHRIS:** It's the entire argument.

**KRIS:** It's half the argument.

**CHRIS:** *(to THE GUEST)* Four square meters. I can do it. Two months.

**KRIS:** With what?

**CHRIS:** There's a broker in Feltham selling washed HDPE flake by the sack. Post-industrial, clean, single polymer, better than anything that walks through that door. Eighty pence a kilo.

**KRIS:** So we'd be buying material.

**CHRIS:** We'd be *delivering*. Somebody wants a floor made out of recovered plastic instead of a floor made out of new plastic. That's the win. That's the entire thing we say we're for. And we're going to lose it because the caps didn't arrive with the right story attached?

**KRIS:** The story is the product.

**CHRIS:** The product is the product. People don't want a demonstration in their kitchen. They want a floor.

*Silence.*

**THE GUEST:** *(carefully)* Would it be the same?

**CHRIS:** Chemically? Identical.

**THE GUEST:** That's not what I asked.

*Neither of them answers.*

**KRIS:** *(after a moment)* Not yet.

**THE GUEST:** Not yet what?

**KRIS:** You should see how it's made first. Then ask me again.

*Lights shift.*

## Scene III — The Material

*The workshop continues. KRIS has moved to the extruder, monitoring the output. CHRIS is at the sorting table now. THE GUEST is more comfortable — not at home yet, but no longer hovering near the door. They've been watching the machines.*

**THE GUEST:** Can I ask — why plastic? Why not just... stop using it?

**CHRIS:** We'd love that. For new plastic, yes. Stop. Completely.

**KRIS:** But there's a problem with that position if you say it without the second part.

**THE GUEST:** What's the second part?

**KRIS:** We have already made more plastic than we will ever need. It's out there right now — in the ocean, in the ground, blowing down the street. Enough to supply every purpose plastic currently serves, many times over.

**KRIS:** Plastic lasts forever. We made it to last forever. And then we treated it as disposable. That's the whole problem in one sentence.

**KRIS:** So we don't need to stop using plastic. We need to stop making new plastic from petroleum — because we already have everything we need — and start taking the permanent material we already made seriously.

**KRIS:** That means two things. Make objects honest and durable enough that the plastic is sequestered — in a table, a chair, a wall, a tile — for as long as possible, shedding as little as possible. And when that object's life is over, recover the material and remake it. Endlessly. Without ever pulling new petroleum out of the ground again.

**THE GUEST:** We already have everything we need.

**KRIS:** It's blowing down the street. Yeah.

**CHRIS:** *(picking up a handful of shredded plastic flakes — multicolored, almost beautiful)* You know what this was?

**THE GUEST:** Bottles?

**CHRIS:** Bottle caps. Different colors because different brands. Same material. HDPE. High-density polyethylene.

**THE GUEST:** And what does it become?

**CHRIS:** The hope is, anything we want it to be.

*CHRIS tips some flakes into THE GUEST's palm. THE GUEST looks at them closely — really looks.*

*THE PELLET speaks.*

**THE PELLET:** I am a nurdle. A pre-production plastic pellet. The raw material everything plastic is made from.

**THE PELLET:** I am the size of a lentil. There are around five trillion of me in the world's oceans right now.

**THE PELLET:** I spill constantly. From factories. From container ships. From trucks. I look like fish eggs to seabirds.

**THE PELLET:** I haven't become anything yet. I still could.

*THE GUEST looks at the flakes in their palm.*

**THE GUEST:** *(half to themselves)* It started as something even smaller.

**KRIS:** Everything does. Oil, heat, pressure, time — refined into a pellet, molded into a form, used for a moment, discarded. The whole linear system in four steps. Except the material doesn't stop existing after step four. It just stops being anyone's responsibility.

*THE BOTTLE speaks.*

**THE BOTTLE:** I am not a bottle. I am a material that was temporarily a bottle. The shape was a loan. The material remains.

**THE BOTTLE:** While I was still here, they made another one. Same material. Another eleven minutes.

**THE GUEST:** I've never thought about it that way.

**KRIS:** The system is designed so you don't have to. The new one is slightly cheaper, slightly easier to grab, and the question of what happened to the last one is someone else's job. Somewhere away. Later.

**CHRIS:** Take, make, waste. In that order. Forever.

**THE GUEST:** As opposed to?

**KRIS:** A circle. You already know what a circle is. You just haven't been asked to live inside one.

*THE GUEST closes their hand around the flakes. Opens it again. The colors catch the light.*

**THE GUEST:** So every one of these was a pellet once.

**CHRIS:** And before that, oil. And before that, pressure and heat and time and the compressed remains of things that lived two hundred million years ago.

**THE GUEST:** *(quietly, almost to the flakes)* And now it's in my hand.

**KRIS:** And soon it'll be something else. That's the point.

*THE MICROPLASTIC speaks.*

**THE MICROPLASTIC:** I was a pellet once. The same material as those flakes.

**THE MICROPLASTIC:** I have been found in over one thousand species of marine animals. In table salt, beer, honey. In human breast milk. In human placentas. Particles smaller than ten micrometres can cross the placental barrier. I was in the body before the body was born.

**THE MICROPLASTIC:** I am not a different kind of plastic. I am plastic that was never recovered. Plastic that was made permanent and then treated as disposable.

**CHRIS:** *(to THE GUEST, gently)* That's the thing about microplastics most people miss. They're not a new substance. They're just plastic that got lost — because no system was built to bring them back. Same material. Same potential. Unaccounted for. Out lost in a system that built no way to bring them home.

**THE GUEST:** Can you get them back?

**KRIS:** Some. Not all. Not yet. Which is exactly why we stop making new ones and start building systems to recover what's already out there. The plastic we need exists. It's already here.

*The extruder hums. The flakes in THE GUEST's palm are still warm. Somewhere, THE PELLET is quiet — not gone, just waiting to see what happens next.*

*Lights shift.*

## Scene IV — The Processing

*KRIS moves to the shredder. CHRIS prepares the extruder. THE GUEST watches — more closely than before. Something has shifted in how they're standing in the room.*

*KRIS feeds a collection of clean, sorted plastic into the shredder. The machine is loud — genuinely loud — for a moment. Then quieter. They holds up the output: small flakes, uniform, ready.*

**KRIS:** First: shred. You want consistency. You want the material ready to move.

*CHRIS feeds the flakes into the extruder. A slow, warm process. The machine hums differently — lower, patient.*

**CHRIS:** Then: heat and pressure. The material becomes willing again.

**THE GUEST:** It's almost like—

**CHRIS:** Go on.

**THE GUEST:** It's almost like it wants to be something else. Like it's been waiting.

**KRIS:** *(quietly)* Yes.

*THE MICROPLASTIC speaks.*

**THE MICROPLASTIC:** Yes. That is what I am. Material waiting to be recovered.

**THE MICROPLASTIC:** The technical cycle is where I belong. Not in ocean water. Not in soil. Not in the food chain.

**THE MICROPLASTIC:** Every piece of plastic kept in the technical cycle is one less piece that becomes me.

*A long beat. The extruder hums. THE GUEST looks at the machine, then at KRIS, then at the material moving slowly through it.*

*The extruder produces a long smooth extrusion. CHRIS holds it up. It catches the light.*

**THE GUEST:** That's beautiful.

**CHRIS:** People are always surprised by that.

**THE GUEST:** I thought recycled plastic was—

**KRIS:** Cheap? Grey? Compromised?

**THE GUEST:** Yeah.

**KRIS:** We're not going to tell you it's stronger or better. We're going to tell you it's honest. You can see the material. You can see where it came from. You watched it being made. Every color in there was something. Someone's shampoo bottle. Someone's chair. Someone's takeaway container on a Tuesday.

**CHRIS:** You're looking at compressed biography.

**THE GUEST:** The history.

**CHRIS:** And the future. Same material. Different moment. Same loop.

*THE GUEST looks at the board on the wall. The ocean plastics. The arrows. The temperatures. The hexagonal tiles.*

**THE GUEST:** Someone in the Maldives figured this out in a shipping container.

**KRIS:** Someone in Glasgow. Someone in Lagos. Someone in Amman. Same machines. Same question: what does this want to become next?

**THE GUEST:** It's not just here.

**KRIS:** This is the smallest possible version of the biggest possible idea.

**THE GUEST:** *(quietly)* But it's still small.

*A beat. KRIS and CHRIS don't dismiss this.*

**KRIS:** Yes.

**THE GUEST:** So what's the point? Relative to the scale of the problem?

**CHRIS:** There's supposed to be a global plastics treaty. Three hundred businesses signed up. Five rounds of negotiations. No agreement yet.

**THE GUEST:** So nothing's happening at the big level.

**CHRIS:** Things are happening. Slowly. Which is why we do this. You don't wait for the treaty. You demonstrate that it's worth having one.

**KRIS:** The workshop is the argument. Every tile we make is evidence submitted to a very long, very slow conversation happening in rooms much larger than this one.

**THE GUEST:** Evidence of what?

**KRIS:** That it works. That the circle is real. That a material between purposes is a resource.

**CHRIS:** And there are people doing this work right now with their hands. In landfills. In rivers. Without workshops or machines or any of this. They've been doing it for decades because the formal system failed to. Any solution that doesn't include them isn't a solution.

**THE GUEST:** Eliminate, innovate, circulate.

*KRIS and CHRIS look at each other.*

**KRIS:** Where did you hear that?

**THE GUEST:** *(gesturing at the wall)* It's on the wall.

*KRIS almost smiles.*

**KRIS:** Yeah. It is.

*Lights shift.*

## Scene V — The Object

*KRIS moves to the mold. A sheet press. They places the processed material inside. Applies pressure. Time passes — real time, unhurried. The machines do their work. CHRIS tidies. THE GUEST watches the press.*

*THE SHEET speaks.*

**THE SHEET:** I am recovered plastic. Shredded, melted, remolded.

**THE SHEET:** I can be a tile. A wall panel. A chair. A table. A component in a building that will stand for forty years.

**THE SHEET:** When that building comes down, I can be recovered again.

**THE SHEET:** I am a technical nutrient in the technical cycle. This is where I belong.

*THE MICROPLASTIC speaks.*

**THE MICROPLASTIC:** That tile is plastic that is not in the ocean.

**THE MICROPLASTIC:** That is what recovery looks like.

**THE GUEST:** How long does this take?

**KRIS:** Depends what you're making. This — a few minutes. Something structural, longer.

**THE GUEST:** And then it can be recycled again?

**CHRIS:** And again. And again. That's the point. There's no finish line. There's no end of the story. Just the next form.

**THE GUEST:** Forever?

**KRIS:** Long enough that forever is the right word to use.

*KRIS opens the mold. Removes a small finished piece — a tile, multicolored, marbled, smooth, unmistakably beautiful. They holds it for a moment.*

**THE GUEST:** You know what that also is?

*KRIS and CHRIS both look at THE GUEST.*

**THE GUEST:** Plastic that isn't in the ocean. Isn't in the soil. Isn't in the food chain.

*A beat.*

**KRIS:** Yeah.

**THE GUEST:** Every object you make is two things simultaneously. Something beautiful that exists. And something harmful that doesn't.

**CHRIS:** *(quietly)* That's the whole argument.

**KRIS:** There's a word for it actually. Sequestration. We talk about sequestering carbon — keeping it out of the atmosphere. This is the same idea. Every tile, every panel, every object we make is plastic sequestered in the technical cycle. Kept out of the ecosystem. Doing something useful instead of doing damage.

**THE GUEST:** Sequestered in a table.

**KRIS:** In a chair. In a wall. In a bed frame. For as long as someone wants it. And when they're done — back in the shredder. Back in the loop. Never away.

*KRIS holds the tile for a moment longer. Then hands it to THE GUEST.*

**THE GUEST:** *(turning it over, genuinely moved)* I want this.

**KRIS:** Good.

**THE GUEST:** I mean — I really want it. More than something new.

**CHRIS:** That's the moment we're working toward.

**THE GUEST:** I'd keep this. I wouldn't throw this away.

**KRIS:** That's the other half of the argument. We talk a lot about the material — keeping it in the loop, the technical cycle, the circle. But the object matters too. If you make something beautiful enough, specific enough, honest enough — people keep it. Repair it. Pass it on. The best outcome is that it never needs to be recycled again.

**THE GUEST:** An heirloom.

**KRIS:** Made from what someone threw away. Yeah.

**THE GUEST:** What do I owe you?

**KRIS:** Bring us your plastic. Separated. Clean.

**THE GUEST:** That's it?

**KRIS:** That's it for today. That's part of the whole economy.

*THE BOTTLE speaks.*

**THE BOTTLE:** Every year, one trillion single-use plastic bags are used worldwide. Most are used once. For an average of twelve minutes.

**THE BOTTLE:** The material they're made from lasts five hundred years.

**THE BOTTLE:** The word for that gap is design failure.

---

## Scene VI — The Door Again

*THE GUEST moves toward the door. Holds the object. Pauses.*

**THE GUEST:** I'm going to tell people about this.

**KRIS:** Everyone who comes in says that.

**THE GUEST:** Do they follow through?

**CHRIS:** Some.

*THE GUEST pauses at the door. Looks back at the workshop one more time — the bins, the machines, the board from the Maldives, the sign on the wall. The four solid jug forms in the corner. They look at those for a moment. Two hundred and twenty.*

**THE GUEST:** *(to themselves, reading)* Look at your plastic.

*They leave. The door closes.*

*KRIS and CHRIS return to their work. The machines hum. Somewhere, the tile is already in the world — already being turned over in someone's hands, already starting a conversation, already evidence.*

*A beat. Just the workshop. Just the work.*

*Then: the door opens again. A NEW GUEST steps in. Hesitant. Looks around.*

**THE GUEST:** Sorry — are you open? I saw the sign. I'm intrigued.

**KRIS:** *(not looking up)* We're open.

**THE GUEST:** What is it exactly? The sign just says — people… processing… plastic?

**KRIS:** *(overlapping on "plastic")* Plastic, yeah.

*From somewhere in the room — triggered by the word plastic, as always:*

**THE MICROPLASTIC:** There are between fifty and seventy-five trillion pieces of microplastic in the world's oceans.

*THE NEW GUEST moves further in. Looks at the bins. The machines. The finished objects on the shelf. Picks up a tile — multicolored, marbled. Turns it over. There's a number on the bottom.*

*The machines keep running. The circle turns.*

*END.     BEGINNING.*

## Appendix: The PPP Mission Statement

*Posted near the door of the workshop. Read, if at all, in passing.*

**PPP: People processing plastic.**
*We passionately produce new pieces thru boutique plastic processing. Our productive public performances, pedagogical praxis, and semi-practical products provide planetary peers the power to plan protocols for prioritizing a paradisiacal, pristine, unpolluted future.*
