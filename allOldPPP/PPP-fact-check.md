# People Processing Plastic — Fact Check & Sources

A line-by-line check of every factual claim in the play. Each entry: the claim as written, the verdict, and a citation you can put in the zine.

**Legend:** ✅ supported · ⚠️ needs adjusting · ❌ not supported / change it

---

## 1. "Eleven minutes" — the average use time of a bottle

**In the play:** repeated 3× ("used for eleven minutes… that's the average," and "eleven minutes" twice more).

❌ **No source supports eleven minutes.** The real, citable figures are:

- Single-use plastic broadly / bags: **~12 minutes** average use.
- Plastic bags: **~15 minutes** "working life."
- Coffee cups: **~15 minutes.**
- Bottles: usually described qualitatively as "a few minutes" / "less than five minutes," not a firm averaged number.

**Recommendation:** change "eleven minutes" to **"twelve minutes,"** which is the most widely cited averaged figure for single-use plastic and is defensible. If you specifically want it to be about a *bottle*, say "a few minutes" or switch the object to a bag, where 12 minutes is well-sourced.

> Source: NRDC, *Single-Use Plastics 101* (single-use plastics "meant to be disposed of right after use — often, in mere minutes"). https://www.nrdc.org/stories/single-use-plastics-101
> Source (12 min, bags): New York City Dept. of Sanitation figure, widely cited. National Geographic, *The world's plastic pollution crisis, explained* — single-use items "have a lifespan of mere minutes to hours." https://www.nationalgeographic.com/environment/article/plastic-pollution

*Note: "eleven minutes" has circulated online but I could not trace it to a primary study. Twelve is the safer number.*

## 2. Material lasts 400–500 years

**In the play:** "will still exist in four hundred years"; "The material they're made from lasts five hundred years."

✅ **Supported.** NOAA / Woods Hole Sea Grant marine-debris breakdown estimates: **plastic bottles ~450 years**, fishing line ~600 years, bags ~20 years.

> Source: World Economic Forum summarising NOAA & Woods Hole data. https://www.weforum.org/stories/2018/11/chart-of-the-day-this-is-how-long-everyday-plastic-items-last-in-the-ocean/

**Recommendation:** "four hundred years" and "five hundred years" are both within the cited range for bottles/diapers. Fine as written, or standardise on **450 years** to match NOAA exactly.

## 3. A drinks bottle is PET / resin code 1

✅ **Correct.** Clear drinks bottles are polyethylene terephthalate (PET), Resin Identification Code #1.

> Source: Veolia / Up To Us, *24 hours in the life of a plastic bottle*. https://www.up-to-us.veolia.com/en/recycling/plastic-bottle-PET-history-recycling-polyethylene-terephthalate

**Note:** you already corrected the earlier "polyethylene" slip to PET. Good — a drinks bottle is PET (#1); a *milk jug* is HDPE (#2). The play uses both correctly now (bottles = PET, caps & jugs = HDPE).

---

## 4. Milk jug is HDPE #2; caps are HDPE

✅ **Correct.** Milk jugs are HDPE (#2). Most bottle caps are HDPE or PP (#5). The play treats caps as HDPE, which is the common case — defensible, though some caps are PP. If you want to be unimpeachable, "HDPE or polypropylene" covers it.

## 5. One laundry load releases up to 700,000 microplastic fibres

✅ **Fully supported — this is your strongest citation.** Peer-reviewed:

> **Napper, I. E. & Thompson, R. C. (2016).** "Release of synthetic microplastic plastic fibres from domestic washing machines: Effects of fabric type and washing conditions." *Marine Pollution Bulletin* 112(1–2), 39–45. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.marpolbul.2016.09.025

Exact findings for a 6 kg load: polyester-cotton **~138,000** fibres, polyester **~496,000**, acrylic **~729,000**. "Over 700,000 fibres" refers to the acrylic figure. ✅ accurate as "up to 700,000."

## 6. 52,000 fibres a year from one shirt washed fortnightly

⚠️ **Plausible arithmetic but I have not found a single primary source stating this exact number.** It appears to be a derived figure (per-wash ÷ garment × washes/year). It's in the right order of magnitude but is a secondary/derived stat.

**Recommendation:** either cite it as "an estimated 52,000 fibres a year" and footnote it as derived, or drop it and lean on the sourced 700,000-per-load figure, which is airtight. *(This line may already be cut in v6 — check.)*

## 7. 60% of clothing fibre is plastic / synthetic textiles = 35% of ocean microplastics

⚠️ **Directionally correct, numbers vary by source.**

- Synthetic fibres are ~60–65% of global textile production — ✅ widely cited (Textile Exchange).
- "35% of ocean microplastics from textiles" traces to the **IUCN (2017)** *Primary Microplastics in the Oceans* report, which attributes **~35% of primary microplastics** to synthetic textiles. ✅ citable, but note it's *primary* microplastics, not all ocean microplastics.

> Source: IUCN (2017), Boucher & Friot, *Primary Microplastics in the Oceans: A Global Evaluation of Sources.* https://portals.iucn.org/library/node/46622

**Recommendation:** phrase as "about a third of *primary* microplastics in the ocean come from synthetic textiles" to match IUCN precisely.

## 8. Microplastics in blood, lungs, placenta, breast milk

✅ **All supported by peer-reviewed studies:**

- **Blood:** Leslie et al. (2022), *Environment International* — first detection of plastic particles in human blood, ~80% of subjects. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.envint.2022.107199
- **Placenta:** Ragusa et al. (2021), *Environment International*, "Plasticenta." https://doi.org/10.1016/j.envint.2020.106274
- **Breast milk:** Ragusa et al. (2022), *Polymers* 14(13):2700. https://doi.org/10.3390/polym14132700
- **Lungs:** Jenner et al. (2022), *Science of the Total Environment.* https://doi.org/10.1016/j.scitotenv.2022.154907

## 9. Particles <10 µm cross the placental barrier / "in the body before the body was born"

✅ **Supported.** Placental studies confirm MPs on both maternal and fetal sides; in-vitro work shows 5 µm polystyrene particles cross/affect placental tissue. Systematic reviews confirm MPs (2.1–100 µm) in placenta and fetal body, with correlations to reduced birthweight and gestational age.

> Source: Frontiers in Endocrinology (2024), PS-MPs on placental explants. https://doi.org/10.3389/fendo.2024.1481014
> Source: Systematic review, PMC. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11186737/

**Note:** the play's phrasing "in the body before the body was born" is a poetic gloss on real evidence of in-utero exposure. Defensible.

---

## 10. Associated with oxidative stress, inflammation, neurotoxic & reproductive effects

✅ **Supported as association, and the play correctly hedges ("the evidence is still building").** Reviews link MP exposure to oxidative stress, inflammation, endocrine disruption, and reproductive/developmental effects — largely from in-vitro and animal models, with human evidence emerging.

> Source: Health Implications of Microplastic Exposure review, PMC. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12412761/

**Recommendation:** keep the hedge. Do not state these as proven human clinical outcomes — the science isn't there yet, and the play's "the evidence is still building; the particles are already there" is exactly the right register.

## 11. 50–75 trillion microplastic pieces in the ocean

⚠️ **Range varies wildly by study — pick your number carefully.**

- The **50–75 trillion** figure is citable (Eunomia / various syntheses).
- A more recent, higher-profile figure is **~170 trillion particles** (Eriksen et al., 2023, *PLOS ONE*).

> Source (170 trillion): Eriksen et al. (2023), *PLOS ONE.* https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0281596

**Recommendation:** either keep "between fifty and seventy-five trillion" (defensible, conservative) or update to "more than 170 trillion" with the Eriksen cite, which is newer and headline-grade. Don't mix them.

## 12. Single-stream: caps too small, fall through screens, become residue

✅ **Supported.** Small items (loose caps especially) fall through disc screens at MRFs and report to residue/landfill. This is why many programs say "leave caps on bottles" — a loose cap is lost.

> Source: reporting on MRF sorting losses of small-format items (multiple; e.g., Recycling Today, container/cap sorting). General principle well documented in MRF operations literature.

**Recommendation:** solid, but this is the one where a named municipal or industry source in the zine would be worth adding. The mechanism (disc screens, 2-inch minus fines) is standard MRF engineering.

## 13. Glass shatters in single stream; contamination; maintenance costs up 300%+

⚠️ **The mechanism is right; the "300%" needs a firmer home.**

- Broken glass contaminating paper/plastic bales in single-stream is **well documented** ✅.
- "Most single-stream residue is glass (70–85%)" ✅ appears in industry analyses.
- "Maintenance costs up over 300%" — I've seen this cited but could not pin it to one authoritative primary source in this pass. Treat as **needs a citation** before print.

**Recommendation:** keep the glass-contamination point (strong). For the 300% figure, either source it firmly or soften to "sharply increased maintenance costs."

## 14. ~15% of plastic packaging recycled; ~40% mismanaged

✅ **Supported.** These are the headline figures from the Pew / SYSTEMIQ *Breaking the Plastic Wave* (2020) analysis, widely used by the Ellen MacArthur Foundation.

> Source: Pew Charitable Trusts & SYSTEMIQ (2020), *Breaking the Plastic Wave.* https://www.pewtrusts.org/en/research-and-analysis/articles/2020/07/23/breaking-the-plastic-wave-top-findings

Also: **only ~9% of all plastic ever made has been recycled** (OECD 2022) — a stronger, even more citable version if you want it.

> Source: OECD (2022), *Global Plastics Outlook.* https://www.oecd.org/environment/plastic-pollution-is-growing-relentlessly-as-waste-management-and-recycling-fall-short.htm

## 15. UK household: 60 pieces of plastic packaging per week / ~90 billion a year

✅ **Supported — this is a real, well-run citizen-science figure.**

> Source: **The Big Plastic Count** (Greenpeace UK & Everyday Plastic). 2022 result: households threw away an average of **60 pieces** of plastic packaging per week → projected ~90 billion pieces/year UK. 2024 result: ~**59% incinerated, 16% recycled.** https://thebigplasticcount.com

**Recommendation:** attribute it — "the Big Plastic Count found…" — both because it's true and because it points the audience at a thing they can actually do.

## 16. Disposal fates: 58% burnt, 17% recycled, rest exported/landfilled

⚠️ **Close to the Big Plastic Count figures but check the year.**

- **2022 Big Plastic Count:** ~46% incinerated, 25% landfilled, 17% exported, 12% recycled (varies by how reported).
- **2024 Big Plastic Count:** **59% incinerated, 16% recycled**, remainder landfilled/exported.

**Recommendation:** the play says "58% burnt / 17% recycled." That's very close to the **2024** figure (59% burnt / 16% recycled). **Update to 59% and 16%** and cite Big Plastic Count 2024, or say "roughly three-fifths burnt, about one in six recycled" to stay robust as figures update.

## 17. Milk-jug maths: 55 jugs ≈ one solid jug ≈ weight of a full jug

✅ **Arithmetic checks out** (verified earlier in our work):
- 1-gallon jug interior ≈ 3.785 L; HDPE density ≈ 0.95 g/cm³ → solid mass ≈ **3.6 kg (7.9 lb)**.
- Empty jug ≈ 65 g → **~55 jugs** per solid jug. Four legs → **~220 jugs**, ~14.4 kg / 31.7 lb.
- A full gallon of milk ≈ 3.9 kg / 8.6 lb ≈ the solid jug's weight. ✅ the "same as a full one" line is true.

*No external citation needed — it's a physical calculation. Show the working in the zine.*

## 18. Injection/extrusion temps (~200–220°C); marine rope at 220°C

✅ **Consistent with Precious Plastic practice.** HDPE/PP process in roughly the 160–220°C range depending on machine and polymer; the 220°C figure for recovered marine rope comes from Precious Plastic community research (Paul & Nate, Maldives).

> Source: Precious Plastic community research, "Make things from ocean plastic." https://community.preciousplastic.com/research/make-things-from-ocean-plastic

*(The rope reference may be trimmed in v6 — if it stays, this covers it.)*

## 19. "Harder than virgin" — correctly disputed on stage

✅ **Good that the play rejects this.** Recycled HDPE/PP is generally **equal or slightly inferior** in mechanical properties to virgin, and worse if polymers are mixed. Kris is right to correct Chris. Do **not** claim recycled is stronger — it isn't.

> Source: general polymer literature on recyclate mechanical properties (reprocessing reduces molecular weight / chain length over cycles).
