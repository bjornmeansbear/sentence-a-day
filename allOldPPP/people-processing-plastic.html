People wander in.
Conversations happen.
Someone leaves changed.
The door opens again.
A play in six scenes

People Processing Plastic

A note on production This play is designed to be performed in a Precious Plastic workshop or any space where plastic is visibly being processed. The machines — shredder, extruder, mold — are not props. They work. The sounds are real. The smell of warm plastic is real. The object made during the performance is real, and at the end, it is given away.

The plastic characters may be voices offstage, projections, or simply the material itself — held up, illuminated, allowed to speak. Directors should resist the urge to costume them. They are not metaphors dressed as people. They are the thing itself, given language.

The Microplastic in particular should never feel like a threat or a horror. It is not an accusation. It is an appeal. It has been waiting a long time and it is still, despite everything, willing.

The play should feel like a conversation that got interesting. Not a lecture. Never a lecture.
Cast of Characters
MARA Workshop founder. Has been doing this for years. Unhurried.
DEV Mara's collaborator. Engineer by training. Finds beauty in process.
THE GUEST Wandered in off the street. Curious. Slightly guilty. Doesn't know why they stopped.
THE BOTTLE A voice. Speaks directly to the audience. Was made eleven minutes ago, in the sense that matters.
THE MICROPLASTIC A voice. Small. Dispersed. Has been everywhere and belongs nowhere — not because of anything it did, but because no system was built to bring it home. Speaks with longing, not accusation.
THE SHEET A voice. Calm. Certain of its worth. The thing that will be made. The thing everything has been moving toward.
Scene I
The Door
[A workshop. Workbenches, tools, machines. A shredder. An extruder. Molds on shelves. Plastic — sorted by type, by color — in bins and bags and buckets. Everything has a place. It is warm in here. It smells faintly of something industrial becoming something else.]
[MARA is at a workbench. DEV is checking the extruder. Neither of them is performing busyness. They are simply working.]
[THE BOTTLE speaks — from somewhere, from everywhere, from the bin by the door.]
THE BOTTLE
I was made this morning. Or eleven years ago. Time moves differently when you're designed to be thrown away.
THE BOTTLE
They used me for eleven minutes. I know because that's the average. Eleven minutes. Then I became a problem.
THE BOTTLE
But here's what they don't tell you at the factory:
THE BOTTLE
I could be anything. I have been anything. Oil, once. Heat, once. The inside of a mountain, once, if you're patient about time. I am not disposable. I am infinitely willing to become.
[The door opens. THE GUEST steps in, hesitant. Looks around.]
THE GUEST
Sorry — is this open? I saw the sign.
MARA[not looking up immediately]
It's always open.
THE GUEST
What is it exactly? The sign just said—
MARA
Plastic workshop. Come in. You don't have to do anything.
[THE GUEST steps further in. Looks at the bins of sorted plastic. Picks up a bottle. Turns it over.]
THE GUEST
Is this all... rubbish?
DEV[mildly]
That word does a lot of damage, yeah.

Scene II
The Misclassification
THE GUEST
What do you do with it?
MARA
What do you think we do with it?
THE GUEST
Recycle it?
MARA
That word also does a lot of damage.
THE GUEST
How so?
DEV
Recycling, the way most people mean it — put it in the blue bin, feel better, don't think about it again — that's just outsourcing the guilt. Half of it goes to landfill anyway. More than half, depending on the year and the country and the mood of the market.
THE GUEST
So it doesn't work.
MARA
The bin system doesn't work. The material works fine.
[THE MICROPLASTIC speaks. Not from the walls, not from the air — from somewhere specific and small. A shaft of light, perhaps. A single point in the room. The voice is not frightening. It is patient. It has been waiting a long time to be heard.]
THE MICROPLASTIC
I want to be a table.
THE MICROPLASTIC
I want to be a chair someone sits in for thirty years. A wall panel. A beam. A tile under someone's feet every morning.
THE MICROPLASTIC
I am small enough to be anything.
THE MICROPLASTIC
I have been in the ocean. In ice. In a fish, then in the person who ate the fish. Not because I wanted to be there. Because no one built a door for me to come back through.
THE MICROPLASTIC
I am not haunting you.
THE MICROPLASTIC
I am asking.
[A beat. The machines hum.]
THE GUEST[quietly]
What is that?
MARA
The part of the story no one tells properly.
DEV
Microplastics aren't a monster. They're a material that fell through every crack we built into the system. Polymer chains, wandering. Looking for somewhere to belong.
THE GUEST
That's — a generous way to think about it.
MARA
It's an accurate way. The problem was never the plastic. The problem was always the design of the system around it.
THE GUEST
So what's different about here?
MARA
Here we know what we have. People bring us specific things. HDPE. PET. Polypropylene. They separate it at home, in their office, at the café. It arrives clean. It arrives sorted. We don't have to undo anyone's mess.
DEV
The big system collects everything together and tries to sort it out later. Which is expensive and mostly doesn't work. We collect it already sorted. Because the people doing the collecting — they know what they have.
THE GUEST
That sounds... simple.
MARA
Most true things are.
Scene 2 of 6
Scene III
The Material
THE GUEST
Can I ask — why plastic? Why not just... stop using it?
DEV
We'd love that. For new plastic, yes. Stop. Completely.
MARA
But there's a problem with that position if you say it without the second part.
THE GUEST
What's the second part?
MARA
All the plastic we could ever need already exists. It's out there right now. In the ocean. In the ground. Floating in various forms. We don't need to make a single new piece. We just need to stop pretending the old stuff has no value.
DEV[picking up a handful of shredded plastic flakes — multicolored, almost beautiful]
You know what this was?
THE GUEST
Bottles?
DEV
Bottle caps. Different colors because different brands. Same material. HDPE. High-density polyethylene.
THE GUEST
And what does it become?
DEV
Anything we want it to be.
[THE BOTTLE speaks again, warmer now.]
THE BOTTLE
This is what I've been trying to tell you.
THE BOTTLE
I am not a bottle. I was never really a bottle. I was a material that agreed, temporarily, to be a bottle. The shape was a loan. The substance is mine to keep.
THE BOTTLE
They made another me today. While I was still here.
THE BOTTLE
That is the part I find most bewildering.
THE GUEST
I've never thought about it that way.
MARA
Most people haven't. Because the system is designed so you don't have to. Designed so the new one is slightly cheaper, slightly easier to grab, and the question of what happened to the last one is someone else's job, somewhere else, later.
DEV
Linear thinking. Take, make, waste. In that order, every time, forever.
THE GUEST
As opposed to.
MARA
Circle. You already know what a circle is. You just haven't been asked to live inside one.
[THE MICROPLASTIC speaks again. Still patient. But there is something new in it now — not urgency exactly, but proximity. As if it has moved closer.]
THE MICROPLASTIC
I have always been willing.
THE MICROPLASTIC
I am a polymer chain. I can link with others. I can become structure, density, surface, form. I contain the memory of oil and heat and pressure and time.
THE MICROPLASTIC
I did not choose to be small.
THE MICROPLASTIC
I was made large and then abandoned into smallness.
THE MICROPLASTIC
There is a difference.
DEV[to THE GUEST, gently]
That's the thing about microplastics that most people miss. They're not a new substance. They're just plastic that got lost. Same material. Same potential. Just — unaccounted for. Circulating in a system that built no way to bring them back.
THE GUEST
Can you get them back?
MARA
Some. Not all. Not yet. Which is exactly why we should stop making new ones and start building the systems to recover what's already out there.
Scene 3 of 6
Scene IV
The Processing
[MARA moves to the shredder. Feeds in a collection of clean, sorted plastic. The machine is loud — genuinely loud — for a moment. Then quieter. She holds up the output: small flakes, uniform, ready.]
MARA
First: shred. You want consistency. You want the material ready to move.
[DEV feeds the flakes into the extruder. A slow, warm process. The machine hums differently — lower, patient.]
DEV
Then: heat and pressure. The material becomes willing again. It remembers how to move.
THE GUEST
It's almost like—
DEV
Go on.
THE GUEST
It's almost like it wants to be something else. Like it's been waiting.
MARA[quietly]
Yes.
[THE MICROPLASTIC speaks. And now there is something released in the voice — not relief exactly, but recognition. The feeling of being finally, precisely seen.]
THE MICROPLASTIC
Yes.
THE MICROPLASTIC
That is exactly it.
THE MICROPLASTIC
I have been floating for so long.
THE MICROPLASTIC
Not haunting. Not threatening. Just—
THE MICROPLASTIC
waiting to be gathered.
THE MICROPLASTIC
Waiting for someone to say: we see you, we know what you are, we know what you could be.
THE MICROPLASTIC
I want to be part of something.
THE MICROPLASTIC
I have always wanted to be part of something.
THE MICROPLASTIC
The waiting was never mine to do.
THE MICROPLASTIC
That was yours.
[A long beat. The extruder hums. The Guest looks at the machine, then at Mara, then at the material moving slowly through it.]
THE GUEST
How long has this been sitting out there.
THE GUEST
Not a question.
MARA
No.
[The extruder produces a long smooth extrusion of plastic. DEV holds it up. It catches the light.]
THE GUEST
That's beautiful.
DEV
People are always surprised by that.
THE GUEST
I thought recycled plastic was—
MARA
Cheap? Grey? Compromised?
THE GUEST
Yeah.
MARA
That's the other lie. Recovered material isn't inferior. It's the same atoms. The same properties. The difference is entirely in our heads — and in the price the market decides to put on it.
DEV
A recycled plastic tile, properly made, is harder than virgin. More beautiful, because of the color variation. More interesting, because of the history in it.
THE GUEST
The history.
DEV
Every color in there was something. Someone's shampoo bottle. Someone's chair. Someone's takeaway container on a Tuesday. You're looking at compressed biography.
Scene 4 of 6
Scene V
The Object
[MARA moves to the mold. A sheet press. She places the processed material inside. Applies pressure. Time passes — real time, unhurried. The machines do their work.]
[THE SHEET speaks for the first time. Unhurried. Self-possessed. It has known what it was going to become for a long time.]
THE SHEET
I am almost ready.
THE SHEET
I have been a mountain and an ocean and a bottle and a problem and a fragment and a flake and a melt and now—
THE SHEET
I am deciding what to be next.
THE SHEET
A table. A tile. A chair. A surfboard. A wall panel in a building someone will live in for forty years.
THE SHEET
They will not know I was a bottle.
THE SHEET
I will not mind.
[THE MICROPLASTIC speaks one last time. The voice is different now — not fragmented, not waiting. Something settled. Something that has found its direction even if it hasn't yet found its way entirely home.]
THE MICROPLASTIC
I see what is possible now.
THE MICROPLASTIC
I have always been made of the right stuff.
THE MICROPLASTIC
There was just no door.
THE MICROPLASTIC
Build the door.
THE GUEST
How long does this take?
MARA
Depends what you're making. This — a few minutes. Something structural, longer.
THE GUEST
And then it can be recycled again?
DEV
And again. And again. That's the point. There's no finish line. There's no end of the story. Just the next form.
THE GUEST
Forever?
MARA
Long enough that forever is the right word to use.
[MARA opens the mold. Removes a small finished piece — a tile, a coaster, a small panel. Multicolored, marbled, smooth, unmistakably beautiful. She holds it for a moment. Then hands it to THE GUEST.]
THE GUEST[turning it over, genuinely moved]
I want this.
MARA
Good.
THE GUEST
I mean — I really want it. More than something new.
DEV
That's the moment we're working toward. Every time.
THE GUEST
What do I owe you?
MARA
Bring us your plastic. Separated. Clean.
THE GUEST
That's it?
MARA
That's the whole economy.
[THE BOTTLE speaks for the last time. Or the first time. Depending on where you are in the circle.]
THE BOTTLE
I was made this morning.
THE BOTTLE
I will be made again tomorrow.
THE BOTTLE
The word for what I am is not waste.
THE BOTTLE
The word for what I am is—
THE BOTTLE
next.
Scene 5 of 6
Scene VI
The Door Again
[THE GUEST moves toward the door. Holds the object. Pauses.]
THE GUEST
I'm going to tell people about this.
MARA
Everyone who comes in says that.
THE GUEST
Do they follow through?
DEV
Some do. Enough.
MARA
Enough is how circles grow.
[THE GUEST leaves. The door closes. MARA and DEV return to their work. The machines hum. Somewhere, the object is already in the world, already being shown to someone, already starting a conversation.]
[Then: the door opens again. A NEW GUEST steps in. Hesitant. Looks around.]
NEW GUEST
Sorry — is this open? I saw the sign.
MARA[not looking up immediately]
It's always open.
[From somewhere small — the same point of light, the same patient voice:]
THE MICROPLASTIC
I want to be a table.
[Lights hold on the open door. The machines keep running. The circle turns.]
