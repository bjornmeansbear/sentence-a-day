## A Note on Production

This play is designed to be performed in a Precious Plastic workshop — or any space where plastic is visibly being processed. The machines — shredder, extruder, mold — are not props. They work. The sounds are real. The smell of warm plastic is real. The object made during the performance is real, and at the end, it is given away.

On the walls: hand-made materials explaining the types of plastic, their resin codes, what they can become. Nothing glossy. Nothing corporate. The kind of thing someone made themselves, with care, because they wanted people to understand. A circle diagram showing the loop. The words LOOK AT YOUR PLASTIC somewhere visible. A sign near the door: PPP: People Processing Plastic.

The plastic characters — The Bottle, The Pellet, The Microplastic, The Sheet — may be voices offstage, projections, or simply the material itself, held up and illuminated. Directors should resist the urge to costume them. They are not metaphors dressed as people. They are the thing itself, given language.

The Microplastic in particular should never feel like a threat or a horror. It is not an accusation. It is an appeal — patient, gently resigned, quietly persistent. It has been waiting a long time and it is still, despite everything, willing.

We are not anti-plastic. We are anti-misuse of plastic. This is not about celebrating plastic but about challenging its wasteful use and demonstrating that even the most problematic materials can have a meaningful place in a circular, regenerative system.

The play should feel like a conversation that got interesting. Not a lecture. Never a lecture.

## Cast of Characters

**KRIS** — Workshop founder. Has been doing this for years. Unhurried. Thinks in systems but speaks in specifics.

**CHRIS** — Kris's collaborator. Engineer by training. Finds beauty in process. Knows the temperature at which everything melts.

**THE GUEST** — Wandered in off the street. Curious. Slightly guilty. Doesn't know why they stopped.

**THE NEW GUEST** — Arrives at the end. Identical entrance. The circle turning.

**THE BOTTLE** — A voice. Wry. Bewildered by its own disposability. Speaks directly to the audience. Was made eleven minutes ago, in the sense that matters.

**THE PELLET** — A voice. Young. Slightly nervous. Pure potential that doesn't yet know what the world will do with it.

**THE MICROPLASTIC** — A voice. Has been everywhere and belongs nowhere — not because of anything it did, but because no system was built to bring it home. Speaks with patience and gentle resignation. Eeyore, if Eeyore were a polymer chain.

**THE SHEET** — A voice. Calm. Self-possessed. Certain of its worth. The thing everything has been moving toward.

## Scene I — The Workshop

*A Precious Plastic workshop. A shredder, an extruder warming up, a sorting table covered in plastic by type and color. Bins labeled: HDPE. PET. PP. A shelf of finished objects — tiles, coasters, small panels — multicolored, marbled, quietly beautiful. In one corner, four dense solid forms — the shape of milk jugs, grey-blue and marbled, a handle faintly visible in each — arranged as if holding something up. On the walls: hand-made posters showing plastic types, resin codes, what each becomes — drawn or printed, pinned up with care. A circle diagram. A sign in plain lettering: LOOK AT YOUR PLASTIC. Near the door: PPP: People Processing Plastic.*

*Everything has a place. It is warm. It smells faintly of something industrial becoming something else.*

*KRIS is at the sorting table, working through a bag of mixed plastic. CHRIS is at the shredder, feeding in a steady stream of bottle caps — red, blue, white. Neither of them is performing busyness. They are simply working.*

*THE BOTTLE speaks. From somewhere in the room. From the bin by the door. From everywhere and nowhere particular.*

**THE BOTTLE:** I was made this morning. Or eleven years ago. Time moves differently when you're thought of as disposable — but designed from materials that can literally last forever.

**THE BOTTLE:** I was used for eleven minutes. I know because that's the average bottle's life expectancy. Eleven minutes. Then I became a "problem." I got sent "away."

**THE BOTTLE:** *(a beat)* Nobody asks where "away" is.

**THE BOTTLE:** But here's what they don't tell you at the factory: plastic is amazing… I could be anything. I have been anything. Oil, once. A Chair, maybe. A Dinosaur, once. A Toy Dinosaur, once. The inside of a mountain, once… a bottle, twice… if you're patient about time. I am not disposable. I am infinitely willing to become.

*The shredder runs. CHRIS adjusts something. KRIS holds up a piece of plastic, reads the number on the bottom, places it in the wrong bin, then moves it to the right one.*

*KRIS turns to face the audience directly. Easy, untheatrical. Like they does this every day. Because they does.*

**KRIS:** Good afternoon. Welcome to the People Processing Plastic workshop.

*They gestures at the space. The machines. The bins. CHRIS, who doesn't look up.*

**KRIS:** We're open. Don't mind the noise.

*They turns back to their sorting. A moment of just — work. The shredder. The hum of the extruder warming up. Then:*

**KRIS:** *(to CHRIS, not looking up)* That bag's mixed. Sort it before it goes in.

**CHRIS:** It's mostly HDPE.

**KRIS:** The number on the bottom doesn't say "mostly."

**CHRIS:** *(still feeding the shredder)* Mostly is a perfectly good word.

**KRIS:** Not in here it isn't.

*CHRIS pulls the bag. Sorts it. Doesn't complain. This has happened before.*

**KRIS:** Every piece has a grade. You keep the grades separate, you keep the value. Mix them up and you've got —

**CHRIS:** Landfill.

**KRIS:** Landfill. Which is just a word for "value we couldn't be bothered to keep."

**CHRIS:** *(holding up a bright red cap)* Someone brought in a whole crate of these. All the same red. You know what that is?

**KRIS:** Ketchup?

**CHRIS:** One brand. Forty years of one family's ketchup habit. In one crate.

**KRIS:** *(genuinely)* Beautiful.

**CHRIS:** *(also genuinely)* Yeah.

*A beat. Both of them look at the red cap. Then back to work.*

*The door opens. THE GUEST steps in. Hesitant. Looks around.*

**THE GUEST:** Sorry — are you open? I saw the sign. I'm intrigued.

**KRIS:** *(not looking up)* We're open.

**THE GUEST:** What is it exactly? The sign just says — people… processing… plastic?

**KRIS:** *(overlapping on "plastic")* Plastic, yeah.

**KRIS:** We're a plastic workshop. Come in. You don't have to do anything. Let me finish with this — *(gestures vaguely at the sorting)* — and then I can show you some things.

*THE GUEST steps further in. Looks at the bins — labeled HDPE, PET, PP. The machines. The finished objects on the shelf. Picks one up — a small tile, multicolored, marbled. Turns it over. There's a number on the bottom.*

**THE GUEST:** So, you make these?

**CHRIS:** From this. Which was this.

*CHRIS picks up a bottle from the bin. Then holds up a handful of flakes. Then points at the tile in THE GUEST's hands.*

**CHRIS:** Bottle. Flakes. Tile. Same material, three different moments in the same life.

**THE GUEST:** *(looking at the number on the bottom of the tile)* What's this number?

**CHRIS:** Resin identification code. Every piece of plastic has one — molded right into it. Tells you exactly what it's made of. That's a two. HDPE. High-density polyethylene.

**THE GUEST:** So the bins out there—

**CHRIS:** Each bin is one number. One resin. You keep them separate, you keep the value. Mix them and you've got—

**KRIS:** *(from the sorting table, not looking up)* Landfill.

**CHRIS:** The number doesn't lie. The number is the whole story of what something is and what it can become.

**THE GUEST:** Out of... what, trash?

*A small pause. KRIS and CHRIS exchange a look. Not unkind. They've heard this word before.*

**CHRIS:** No. Not trash — waste. There's a difference. Trash is a decision. Waste is material between purposes.

**THE GUEST:** What's the purpose here?

**KRIS:** *(still sorting, picking up a piece, reading the bottom, placing it precisely)* Showing how things could work. Modeling the way we wish things to be. A pragmatic utopian gesture.

*THE GUEST looks at the tile for a long moment. Sets it down carefully. Picks it back up.*

**THE GUEST:** They're beautiful.

**CHRIS:** People say that like it's a surprise.

*THE GUEST doesn't respond. Still looking at the tile. Notices the wall — the plastic types poster, the resin codes 1 through 7, what each one becomes. Reads it.*

**THE GUEST:** So everything plastic has one of these numbers.

**KRIS:** Everything. Look at the bottom of anything plastic in your house. It's there. It's been there the whole time. Most people never look.

**THE GUEST:** *(quietly)* Look at your plastic.

**KRIS:** *(noticing)* Yeah. Exactly.

*THE GUEST looks at the tile for a long moment. Sets it down carefully. Picks it back up.*

**THE GUEST:** They're beautiful.

**CHRIS:** People say that like it's a surprise.

*THE GUEST doesn't respond. Still looking at the tile. Moves further into the space.*

**THE GUEST:** What does it smell like in here?

**CHRIS:** Warm plastic.

**THE GUEST:** Is that... bad?

**CHRIS:** We have ventilation.

**KRIS:** *(dryly, still not looking up)* It smells like something becoming something else. You get used to it. Then you miss it when it's gone.

*THE GUEST absorbs this. Moves along the wall. Stops at a poster — plastic types, samples, arrows, temperatures, what each one becomes. Looks at it closely.*

**THE GUEST:** Marine rope?

**CHRIS:** Was rope. Flows beautifully at 220 degrees apparently. Now it's a tile somewhere.

**THE GUEST:** People don't know rope is plastic?

**KRIS:** Most of them don't. Use it every day. Never once think: this will fray, these fibers will enter the water, this will become something too small to see.

*THE GUEST looks at the poster for a long moment. At the numbers. At the arrows. At what each thing becomes.*

*THE GUEST moves to the circle diagram on the wall.*

**THE GUEST:** And that?

**KRIS:** The circle? It's meant to illustrate a closed loop.

**THE GUEST:** The recycling symbol?

**KRIS:** Sort of, but bigger. Reduce, reuse, recycle… The recycling symbol is superficially just about getting things in the right bin. ultimately, that stuff still goes "away" to who knows where; and is often still "waste" –  This is about never making waste in the first place.

**CHRIS:** Waste equals food.

**THE GUEST:** Sorry — food?

**CHRIS:** Waste from one process becomes the raw material for the next. That's what food is. That's what this is.

**THE GUEST:** So the plastic is food? 

**KRIS:** *(still sorting, not looking up)* The idea is that a material, Plastic in particular, is never finished. That's the whole problem. And the whole point.

*THE GUEST wanders a little more. Still taking it in. Still slightly out of their element — not unwelcome, just in a world whose rules they don't yet know.*

*In a corner of the workshop, something catches their eye. A dense, solid form — roughly the shape of a milk jug but heavy-looking. Grey-blue. Marbled. A handle visible but buried in the mass. They crouch down. Touch it. Try to lift it.*

*It barely moves.*

**THE GUEST:** What is this?

**CHRIS:** *(not looking up)* Milk jugs.

**THE GUEST:** It's—

**CHRIS:** Solid. Yeah.

*A beat. THE GUEST looks at it differently. Near it, three more — four total, arranged as if they might hold something up.*

**THE GUEST:** How many?

**CHRIS:** That one's fifty-five. *(gestures at the others)* Those four together took two hundred and twenty. People kept bringing them in. Couldn't stop once they started.

*THE GUEST straightens up. Looks at the form. Looks at the handle buried in it — present, recognizable, completely non-functional.*

**THE GUEST:** The handle's still there.

**CHRIS:** Yeah.

**THE GUEST:** How much does it weigh?

**CHRIS:** About the same as a full one actually.

*A beat. THE GUEST looks at it again — differently now.*

*And then — from somewhere. Not the walls exactly. A small point in the room. Almost like something that was already there, and just decided to speak.*

**THE MICROPLASTIC:** I've been in this room before, actually.

**THE MICROPLASTIC:** Not this specific room. But rooms like it. Warehouses. Sorting facilities. Places where people almost dealt with me and then didn't quite.

**THE MICROPLASTIC:** It's fine. I'm used to it.

**THE MICROPLASTIC:** *(a beat)* I've been in the North Pacific for eleven years. This is already an improvement.

*THE GUEST pauses. Half-heard something. Looks around.*

**THE GUEST:** *(to KRIS, quietly)* Did you say something?

**KRIS:** *(not looking up)* Hmm?

**THE GUEST:** I thought I heard—

**KRIS:** The extruder makes noises when it's warming up. You stop noticing after a while.

*THE GUEST accepts this. Not entirely convinced. The machines hum. The sorting continues.*

**THE MICROPLASTIC:** *(quietly, to no one in particular)* Nobody ever notices. It's fine. I've been being jetisoned off of clothes, bottles, nets, bouys, for years.

*THE BOTTLE speaks again — from the bin, from the air, from the same everywhere as before. This time like a bridge being built.*

**THE BOTTLE:** The blue bin was supposed to be the answer. The conscience-clearing bin. Put me in there and the problem goes away.

**THE BOTTLE:** Except — *(beat)* — I didn't go away. I went to a sorting facility in another city or country. I got mixed with things I wasn't compatible with. I came back as landfill, or just a chea giant block of mixed plastic. "Away" turned out to be somewhere very specific and very permanent.

**THE BOTTLE:** The bin isn't a circle. It's a very slow, very inefficient line. from make to waste.

*The machines keep running. THE GUEST keeps wandering. Two truths in the same room, not yet fully introduced to each other.*

*Lights shift.*

## Scene II — The Misclassification

*THE GUEST has drifted back toward KRIS and CHRIS. CHRIS is now monitoring the extruder, watching material move through it slowly. KRIS is still sorting — methodical, unhurried.*

**THE GUEST:** So what do you actually do with it all?

**KRIS:** What do you think we do with it?

**THE GUEST:** Recycle it?

**KRIS:** That word isn't our favorite.

**THE GUEST:** Recycling? why?

**CHRIS:** The idea that putting something in a bin is the same as taking responsibility for it. That's sort of what recycling means now, you put a material in the right bin.

**KRIS:** Recycling, the way most people mean it — feel better, don't think about it again — that's outsourcing the guilt. Half of it goes to landfill anyway. More than half, depending on the year, the country, the mood of the commodity market.

**THE GUEST:** So bins don't work?

**KRIS:** The bin system doesn't work. The material works fine. Keeping things separated works fine. That's what people get backwards.

**THE GUEST:** What do you mean backwards?

**CHRIS:** The system decided plastic was low value. Disposable. A problem to get rid of rather than a resource to keep. So it built infrastructure for disposal — single stream bins, trucks, facilities, sorting sites, landfills. Spent billions designing the most efficient possible way to throw something away.

**KRIS:** Instead of building systems to keep it.

**CHRIS:** A polymer chain that will outlast every person in this room by four hundred years. We used it to wrap a sandwich. Then we sent it "away."

**THE GUEST:** When you say it like that it sounds—

**KRIS:** Insane?

**THE GUEST:** I was going to say wasteful.

*KRIS holds up a piece of plastic — turns it in the light.*

**KRIS:** We're not anti-plastic. Plastic is an extraordinary material. Lightweight. Durable. Infinitely formable. It's in medical devices keeping people alive right now. It's in the machines keeping this workshop running. We're anti-misuse. Anti-making something designed to last four centuries and using it for eleven minutes.

*A beat. KRIS turns the piece of plastic over in their hands.*

**KRIS:** If I'm honest — I'd rather not work with plastic at all. If the world had designed better from the start, we wouldn't be here. There'd be no need for this workshop. We'd be working with materials that actually belong in natural cycles.

**KRIS:** But that's not the world we inherited. We inherited this one — saturated with a material that isn't going anywhere. So the question isn't whether to work with it. It's whether to work with it well or badly. With intelligence or without.

**THE GUEST:** And this is working with it well.

**KRIS:** This is trying to. Yeah.

**THE GUEST:** So it's not plastic that's the problem?

**KRIS:** Waste is a design flaw. Not a fact of nature. There is no waste in nature — a leaf falls and feeds the forest. Waste is something we invented. And we can uninvent it.

*THE GUEST moves to the shelf of finished objects. Picks up the tile again. Holds it differently this time — with more attention.*

**THE GUEST:** So these — what are these worth?

**CHRIS:** More than the plastic they came from, according to the market. But the market is wrong about what the plastic was worth in the first place.

**KRIS:** Virgin plastic is cheap because we subsidize extraction. Make it artificially cheap to pull new material out of the ground. The price is a lie. The value was always there — we just decided not to see it.

**THE GUEST:** And recovered plastic?

**KRIS:** Recovered plastic, properly processed, is honest. You can see exactly what it is. You can see where it came from. You watched it being made. Try finding another object in your house you can say that about.

**THE GUEST:** *(looking at the tile)* It's beautiful because you can see what it was.

**CHRIS:** That's it exactly. Virgin plastic is anonymous. This has biography.

*A beat. THE GUEST sets the tile down. Looks around the workshop.*

**THE GUEST:** And the stuff that doesn't make it here? That goes in the bin and doesn't go anywhere useful?

*A pause. KRIS and CHRIS exchange a look.*

**CHRIS:** It gets small.

**THE GUEST:** Small?

**KRIS:** Plastic doesn't biodegrade. It just breaks. Into smaller and smaller pieces. Fragments. Particles. Things too small to sort, too small to collect, too small to see. But still there. Still plastic. Still a technical nutrient in the wrong place.

**THE GUEST:** Technical nutrient?

**CHRIS:** Think about how nature works. A leaf falls. It feeds fungi. Fungi feed the soil. Soil feeds the tree. Nothing is wasted — everything is food for the next process. That's the biological cycle. It's been running for billions of years without a landfill.

**THE GUEST:** And plastic can't do that.

**CHRIS:** Plastic is not biological. You can't compost it. You can't return it to soil. Nature has no mechanism to absorb it — which is why it's still in the ocean after thirty years. It doesn't belong in the biological cycle.

**THE GUEST:** So where does it belong?

**CHRIS:** That's the technical cycle. The economy. The workshop. The loop of making and remaking. Plastic is a technical nutrient — food for manufacturing, food for design, food for the next object. It's a resource the technical system needs to keep running. When it stays in that cycle — shredded, melted, remolded, used again — it has enormous value. It does exactly what it's supposed to do.

**KRIS:** When it leaks out of that cycle — into the ocean, the soil, the food chain — it's not pollution exactly. It's a nutrient in the wrong system. A resource that can't find its way home.

**THE GUEST:** Because the biological cycle can't process it.

**KRIS:** And the technical cycle can't reach it anymore. It's too small. Too dispersed. Too far away.

**CHRIS:** Which is why you keep it in the technical cycle in the first place. Separation at source. Local collection. The workshop. You never let it leave.

**THE GUEST:** The circle.

**KRIS:** The circle.

*THE MICROPLASTIC speaks. From somewhere small and specific. Patient. The voice of something that has been waiting a very long time.*

**THE MICROPLASTIC:** I used to be a table. Something useful. Something that stays in one place. Now I'm in a fish. The fish didn't ask for this either, for what it's worth.

**THE MICROPLASTIC:** *(a beat)* Nobody asks.

**THE MICROPLASTIC:** I've been in the Arctic. In the Mariana Trench. In a cloud, briefly. I didn't enjoy the cloud. Very disorienting. No sense of direction. Lots of other particles, none of them interested in conversation. I have been inside six different humans. None of them knew. I didn't introduce myself. It seemed rude given the circumstances.

**THE MICROPLASTIC:** I'm not complaining.

**THE MICROPLASTIC:** *(long beat)* I'm a little bit complaining.

**THE MICROPLASTIC:** The point is — I didn't end up here because of anything I did. I was made large (a table remember?). Someone used me for like eleven minutes. Nobody built a way to bring me back. So I started to break down, I lost pieces; I got small. And smaller. And now I'm — *(a gesture at everything)* — everywhere. Which sounds impressive I guess, but it is actually quite lonely (and dangerous! i'm bad for all of you!!!).

*A long beat. The machines hum. THE GUEST stands very still.*

**CHRIS:** Microplastics aren't a new substance. They're not alien. They're just plastic that got lost — because no system was designed to bring them home. Polymer chains, wandering. Looking for somewhere to belong.

**THE GUEST:** That's — a generous way to think about it.

**THE MICROPLASTIC:** *(quietly, having overheard)* It is, a bit. Thank you. I appreciate that.

**THE MICROPLASTIC:** Not everyone leads with the generosity. Usually it's — *(affects a slightly alarmed voice)* — "microplastics found in human HEARTS." Which, yes. I know. I was there. I didn't enjoy it either.

**THE GUEST:** *(to KRIS)* The problem was never the plastic.

**KRIS:** The problem was always the design of the system around it.

**THE GUEST:** So what's different here?

**KRIS:** Here we know what we have. People bring us specific things — HDPE, PET, polypropylene. Separated at source. Clean. Sorted. It arrives already knowing what it is. We don't have to undo anyone's mess.

**CHRIS:** The big system collects everything together and tries to sort it out later. Expensive, lossy, mostly doesn't work. We collect it already sorted. Because the people doing the collecting live with the material. They know what they have.

**THE GUEST:** Separation at source.

**KRIS:** Right where you are. Right when you use it. No trucks to faraway facilities. No mixing. No losing the grade.

**THE GUEST:** That sounds simple.

**KRIS:** The linear economy made it complicated on purpose. Complicated meant infrastructure. Infrastructure meant contracts. Contracts meant money. *(beat)* Keeping it simple doesn't make anyone rich enough to build a business around.

**CHRIS:** Except us.

**KRIS:** *(drily)* Except us. Semi-practical products and all.

*THE GUEST almost smiles.*

*Lights shift.*

## Scene III — The Material

*The workshop continues. KRIS has moved to the extruder, monitoring the output. CHRIS is at the sorting table now. THE GUEST is more comfortable — not at home yet, but no longer hovering near the door. They've been watching the machines.*

**THE GUEST:** Can I ask — why plastic? Why not just... stop using it?

**CHRIS:** We'd love that. For new plastic, yes. Stop. Completely.

**KRIS:** But there's a problem with that position if you say it without the second part.

**THE GUEST:** What's the second part?

**KRIS:** All the plastic we could ever need already exists. It's out there right now. In the ocean. In the ground. In the tree tops. Blowing and floating in various forms. We don't need to make a single new piece. We just need to stop pretending the old stuff has no value.

**CHRIS:** *(picking up a handful of shredded plastic flakes — multicolored, almost beautiful)* You know what this was?

**THE GUEST:** Bottles?

**CHRIS:** Bottle caps. Different colors because different brands. Same material. HDPE. High-density polyethylene.

**THE GUEST:** And what does it become?

**CHRIS:** The hope is, anything we want it to be.

*CHRIS tips some flakes into THE GUEST's palm. THE GUEST looks at them closely — really looks.*

*A new voice. Lighter than The Bottle. Younger somehow. A little uncertain — the way potential always is before it knows what it will become.*

**THE PELLET:** I come before all of this.

**THE PELLET:** Before the bottle. Before the cap. Before the film and the bag and the container and the cup. I am the beginning. Well, a post-oil extraction and processing beginning… A small white bead, the size of a lentil, warm from the factory. Pure possibility.

**THE PELLET:** They called me a nurdle. *(a beat)* I didn't choose that name.

**THE PELLET:** I am going to become something. I don't know what. That feels exciting. All that potential. A chair. A medical device. A component in something that saves a life.

**THE PELLET:** Nobody told me some of us don't make it to the mold.

**THE PELLET:** Some of us spill. From the factory. From the container ship. From the truck. We end up on beaches, in rivers, in the bellies of seabirds who thought we looked like fish eggs.

**THE PELLET:** *(quietly)* I just want to become something useful.

*THE GUEST looks at the flakes in their palm.*

**THE GUEST:** *(half to themselves)* It started as something even smaller.

**KRIS:** Everything does. Oil, heat, pressure, time — refined into a pellet, molded into a form, used for a moment, discarded. The whole linear system in four steps. Except the material doesn't stop existing after step four. It just stops being anyone's responsibility.

*THE BOTTLE speaks — warmer now, as if The Pellet's words have reminded it of something it knew before it became a bottle.*

**THE BOTTLE:** This is what I've been trying to tell you.

**THE BOTTLE:** I am not a bottle. I was never really a bottle. I was a material that agreed, temporarily, to be a bottle. The shape was a loan. The substance is mine to keep.

**THE GUEST:** I've never thought about it that way.

**KRIS:** The system is designed so you don't have to. The new one is slightly cheaper, slightly easier to grab, and the question of what happened to the last one is someone else's job. Somewhere away. Later.

**CHRIS:** Take, make, waste. In that order. Forever.

**THE GUEST:** As opposed to?

**KRIS:** A circle. You already know what a circle is. You just haven't been asked to live inside one.

*THE GUEST closes their hand around the flakes. Opens it again. The colors catch the light.*

**THE GUEST:** So every one of these was a pellet once.

**CHRIS:** And before that, oil. And before that, pressure and heat and time and the compressed remains of things that lived two hundred million years ago.

**THE GUEST:** *(quietly, almost to the flakes)* And now it's in my hand.

**KRIS:** And soon it'll be something else. That's the point.

*THE MICROPLASTIC speaks again. Still patient. Closer now — as if The Pellet's arrival has reminded it that it too was once whole and full of promise.*

**THE MICROPLASTIC:** I was a pellet once.

**THE MICROPLASTIC:** I know it doesn't look like it now.

**THE MICROPLASTIC:** I have always been full of polymer potential. Excellent tensile properties, remarkable durability, can be remolded indefinitely. I have a very strong CV for a particle nobody can find.

**THE MICROPLASTIC:** I can link with others. I can become structure, density, surface, form. A small piece of ancient seabed. Some very old sunlight.

**THE MICROPLASTIC:** I was made large and then abandoned into smallness. There is a difference. I feel it's important to maintain that distinction.

**CHRIS:** *(to THE GUEST, gently)* That's the thing about microplastics most people miss. They're not a new substance. They're just plastic that got lost — because no system was built to bring them back. Same material. Same potential. Unaccounted for. Out lost in a system that built no way to bring them home.

**THE GUEST:** Can you get them back?

**KRIS:** Some. Not all. Not yet. Which is exactly why we stop making new ones and start building systems to recover what's already out there. The plastic we need exists. It's already here.

*The extruder hums. The flakes in THE GUEST's palm are still warm. Somewhere, THE PELLET is quiet — not gone, just waiting to see what happens next.*

*Lights shift.*

## Scene IV — The Processing

*KRIS moves to the shredder. CHRIS prepares the extruder. THE GUEST watches — more closely than before. Something has shifted in how they're standing in the room.*

*KRIS feeds a collection of clean, sorted plastic into the shredder. The machine is loud — genuinely loud — for a moment. Then quieter. They holds up the output: small flakes, uniform, ready.*

**KRIS:** First: shred. You want consistency. You want the material ready to move.

*CHRIS feeds the flakes into the extruder. A slow, warm process. The machine hums differently — lower, patient.*

**CHRIS:** Then: heat and pressure. The material becomes willing again.

**THE GUEST:** It's almost like—

**CHRIS:** Go on.

**THE GUEST:** It's almost like it wants to be something else. Like it's been waiting.

**KRIS:** *(quietly)* Yes.

*THE MICROPLASTIC speaks. Something releases in the voice — not relief exactly, but recognition. The feeling of being finally, precisely seen.*

**THE MICROPLASTIC:** Yes.

**THE MICROPLASTIC:** *(a beat, as if surprised by its own agreement)* That is exactly it.

**THE MICROPLASTIC:** I have been floating for — *(considers)* — a very long time. I stopped counting. Counting made it worse.

**THE MICROPLASTIC:** Not threatening. I know I have a reputation. I've seen the headlines. "Microplastics found in human hearts." Yes. I know. I was there. It wasn't my idea.

**THE MICROPLASTIC:** I want to be part of something. I have always wanted to be part of something.

*A long beat. The extruder hums. THE GUEST looks at the machine, then at KRIS, then at the material moving slowly through it.*

*The extruder produces a long smooth extrusion. CHRIS holds it up. It catches the light.*

**THE GUEST:** That's beautiful.

**CHRIS:** People are always surprised by that.

**THE GUEST:** I thought recycled plastic was—

**KRIS:** Cheap? Grey? Compromised?

**THE GUEST:** Yeah.

**KRIS:** We're not going to tell you it's stronger or better. We're going to tell you it's honest. You can see the material. You can see where it came from. You watched it being made. Every color in there was something. Someone's shampoo bottle. Someone's chair. Someone's takeaway container on a Tuesday.

**CHRIS:** You're looking at compressed biography.

**THE GUEST:** The history.

**CHRIS:** And the future. Same material. Different moment. Same loop.

*THE GUEST looks at the board on the wall. The ocean plastics. The arrows. The temperatures. The hexagonal tiles.*

**THE GUEST:** Someone in the Maldives figured this out in a shipping container.

**KRIS:** Someone in Glasgow. Someone in Lagos. Someone in Amman. Same machines. Same question: what does this want to become next?

**THE GUEST:** It's not just here.

**KRIS:** This is the smallest possible version of the biggest possible idea.

**THE GUEST:** *(quietly)* But it's still small.

*A beat. KRIS and CHRIS don't dismiss this.*

**KRIS:** Yes.

**THE GUEST:** So what's the point? Relative to the scale of the problem?

**CHRIS:** There's supposed to be a global plastics treaty. Three hundred businesses signed up. Five rounds of negotiations. No agreement yet.

**THE GUEST:** So nothing's happening at the big level.

**CHRIS:** Things are happening. Slowly. Which is why we do this. You don't wait for the treaty. You demonstrate that it's worth having one.

**KRIS:** The workshop is the argument. Every tile we make is evidence submitted to a very long, very slow conversation happening in rooms much larger than this one.

**THE GUEST:** Evidence of what?

**KRIS:** That it works. That the circle is real. That a material between purposes isn't waste — it's a resource waiting for the right system to claim it.

**CHRIS:** And there are people doing this work right now with their hands. In landfills. In rivers. Without workshops or machines or any of this. They've been doing it for decades because the formal system failed to. Any solution that doesn't include them isn't a solution.

**THE GUEST:** Eliminate, innovate, circulate.

*KRIS and CHRIS look at each other.*

**KRIS:** Where did you hear that?

**THE GUEST:** *(gesturing at the wall)* It's on the wall.

*KRIS almost smiles.*

**KRIS:** Yeah. It is.

*Lights shift.*

## Scene V — The Object

*KRIS moves to the mold. A sheet press. They places the processed material inside. Applies pressure. Time passes — real time, unhurried. The machines do their work. CHRIS tidies. THE GUEST watches the press.*

*THE SHEET speaks for the first time. Unhurried. Self-possessed. It has known what it was going to become for a long time.*

**THE SHEET:** I am almost ready.

**THE SHEET:** I have been a mountain and part of the ocean and a bottle and a problem and a pellet and a nurdle and a fragment and a flake and a melt and now—

**THE SHEET:** I am deciding what to be next.

**THE SHEET:** A table. A tile. A chair. A wall panel in a building someone will live in for forty years. A surf fin made from marine rope by someone in the Maldives who figured out that rope flows beautifully at 220 degrees.

**THE SHEET:** They will not always know I was a bottle.

**THE SHEET:** I will not mind.

**THE SHEET:** I am a technical nutrient. I belong in the loop. I was meant for this.

*THE MICROPLASTIC speaks one last time. The voice is different now — still flat, still resigned, but underneath it, something that has located its direction even if it hasn't yet found the whole road home.*

**THE MICROPLASTIC:** I see what is possible now.

**THE MICROPLASTIC:** I've seen it before, actually. In theory. I've had a lot of time to think, in the gyre.

**THE MICROPLASTIC:** But seeing it — *(beat)* — that's different.

**THE MICROPLASTIC:** I have always been made of the right stuff. I want that noted. The right stuff, in the wrong system. Those are two separate problems and only one of them is mine.

**THE GUEST:** How long does this take?

**KRIS:** Depends what you're making. This — a few minutes. Something structural, longer.

**THE GUEST:** And then it can be recycled again?

**CHRIS:** And again. And again. That's the point. There's no finish line. There's no end of the story. Just the next form.

**THE GUEST:** Forever?

**KRIS:** Long enough that forever is the right word to use.

*KRIS opens the mold. Removes a small finished piece — a tile, multicolored, marbled, smooth, unmistakably beautiful. They holds it for a moment.*

**THE GUEST:** You know what that also is?

*KRIS and CHRIS both look at THE GUEST.*

**THE GUEST:** Plastic that isn't in the ocean. Isn't in the soil. Isn't in the food chain.

*A beat.*

**KRIS:** Yeah.

**THE GUEST:** Every object you make is two things simultaneously. Something beautiful that exists. And something harmful that doesn't.

**CHRIS:** *(quietly)* That's the whole argument.

**KRIS:** There's a word for it actually. Sequestration. We talk about sequestering carbon — keeping it out of the atmosphere. This is the same idea. Every tile, every panel, every object we make is plastic sequestered in the technical cycle. Kept out of the ecosystem. Doing something useful instead of doing damage.

**THE GUEST:** Sequestered in a table.

**KRIS:** In a chair. In a wall. In a bed frame. For as long as someone wants it. And when they're done — back in the shredder. Back in the loop. Never away.

*KRIS holds the tile for a moment longer. Then hands it to THE GUEST.*

**THE GUEST:** *(turning it over, genuinely moved)* I want this.

**KRIS:** Good.

**THE GUEST:** I mean — I really want it. More than something new.

**CHRIS:** That's the moment we're working toward.

**THE GUEST:** I'd keep this. I wouldn't throw this away.

**KRIS:** That's the other half of the argument. We talk a lot about the material — keeping it in the loop, the technical cycle, the circle. But the object matters too. If you make something beautiful enough, specific enough, honest enough — people keep it. Repair it. Pass it on. The best outcome isn't that it gets recycled again quickly. The best outcome is that it never needs to be.

**THE GUEST:** An heirloom.

**KRIS:** Made from what someone threw away. Yeah.

**THE GUEST:** What do I owe you?

**KRIS:** Bring us your plastic. Separated. Clean.

**THE GUEST:** That's it?

**KRIS:** That's it for today. That's part of the whole economy.

*THE BOTTLE speaks for the last time. Or the first time. Depending on where you are in the circle.*

**THE BOTTLE:** I was made this morning.

**THE BOTTLE:** I will be made again tomorrow.

## Scene VI — The Door Again

*THE GUEST moves toward the door. Holds the object. Pauses.*

**THE GUEST:** I'm going to tell people about this.

**KRIS:** Everyone who comes in says that.

**THE GUEST:** Do they follow through?

**CHRIS:** Some.

*THE GUEST pauses at the door. Looks back at the workshop one more time — the bins, the machines, the board from the Maldives, the sign on the wall. The four solid jug forms in the corner. They look at those for a moment. Two hundred and twenty.*

**THE GUEST:** *(to themselves, reading)* Look at your plastic.

*They leave. The door closes.*

*KRIS and CHRIS return to their work. The machines hum. Somewhere, the tile is already in the world — already being turned over in someone's hands, already starting a conversation, already evidence.*

*A beat. Just the workshop. Just the work.*

*Then: the door opens again. A NEW GUEST steps in. Hesitant. Looks around.*

**THE GUEST:** Sorry — are you open? I saw the sign. I'm intrigued.

**KRIS:** *(not looking up)* We're open.

**THE GUEST:** What is it exactly? The sign just says — people… processing… plastic?

**KRIS:** *(overlapping on "plastic")* Plastic, yeah.

*From somewhere small — the same point of light, the same patient voice:*

**THE MICROPLASTIC:** I've been in this room before, actually.

*THE NEW GUEST moves further in. Looks at the bins. The machines. The finished objects on the shelf. Picks up a tile — multicolored, marbled. Turns it over.*

*The machines keep running. The circle turns.*
